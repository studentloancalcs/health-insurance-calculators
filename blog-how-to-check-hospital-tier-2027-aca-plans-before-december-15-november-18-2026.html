How to Check Hospital Tier Placement on 2027 ACA Plans Before December 15 — The In-Network-But-Expensive Trap

By HealthCalc Team

Published November 18, 2026

12 min read

There is a line on the plan details page of HealthCare.gov that reads “In-Network,” and most shoppers stop reading there. A hospital is either in-network or it is not. On a growing share of 2027 ACA Marketplace plans, that line is no longer the whole story. Roughly 35 to 40 percent of 2027 plans now use tiered hospital networks, meaning the in-network list itself is split into two or three cost-sharing categories. One of them, Tier 1, is where the plan wants you to go and has designed its cost-sharing to steer you. The other, Tier 2, is still in-network but carries a deductible, coinsurance, and sometimes an out-of-pocket maximum that can be two to three times higher.

The practical effect is that on a Silver plan that lists both a major academic medical center and the community hospital down the street as “in-network,” a three-day admission at the academic center can cost $12,000 in enrollee out-of-pocket spend while the same admission at the community hospital costs $3,500 — on the same plan, for the same person, with no network-adequacy violation and no surprise billing. The difference is tier placement, and HealthCare.gov does not show it on the shopping page. You have to look it up yourself before December 15, the deadline for January 1, 2027 coverage, or you run the risk of an in-network bill that is not what you expected.

This guide walks through what tiered hospital networks actually are in 2027, which carriers use them, where to find the tier list for any plan, how to verify your hospital's placement in under 10 minutes, what to do when the hospital you want is on Tier 2, and the three mistakes we see every January that cost enrollees four- and five-figure bills.

What a tiered hospital network actually is

A tiered hospital network is a cost-sharing design, not a referral or authorization gate. Every hospital on every tier is in-network. You do not need prior authorization to go to a Tier 2 hospital, and you are protected from balance billing at the hospital's charge-master rate. What you pay the day of discharge is the difference.

Here is what the tiers typically mean in plain language:

  • Tier 1 (Preferred / Select / Enhanced / Value / Centers of Excellence). The carrier's most-favored facilities, usually chosen for a mix of cost efficiency, quality metrics, and negotiated rates. Cost-sharing is the plan's lowest — a smaller deductible, a lower coinsurance percentage, sometimes a flat copay in place of coinsurance.
  • Tier 2 (Standard / Participating). In-network facilities that did not make Tier 1 cuts, often major academic medical centers, teaching hospitals, specialized surgical centers, or hospitals with strong brand names but higher negotiated rates. Cost-sharing is noticeably higher. A separate Tier 2 out-of-pocket maximum is common.
  • Tier 3 (Extended / Access). Used by a smaller number of carriers (Horizon BCBSNJ, Independent Health, and a few others). Technically in-network but with cost-sharing approaching the out-of-network level. Usually added so that a plan can advertise a broad network while steering enrollees away from the highest-cost facilities.
Key idea: “In-network” is a yes/no answer to balance-billing protection. “Tier” is the answer to what your admission will actually cost. The two are not the same question.

Which carriers use tiered hospital networks in 2027

Based on 2027 state rate filings and benefit summaries submitted to CMS, the following carriers offer at least one tiered hospital product on 2027 Marketplace plans in some or all of their markets. The list is directional, not exhaustive, and plan designs vary significantly by state.

  • Aetna / CVS Health. Expanded “Aetna Whole Health” narrow tier for 2027 in Texas, Georgia, and Florida Bronze and Silver plans.
  • Anthem Blue Cross Blue Shield. “Pathway” and “Essential Choice” tier structures across multiple states, with new Tier 1 designations for 2027 in Indiana, Ohio, Kentucky, and Virginia.
  • Blue Shield of California. “Tandem PPO” and “Access+ HMO” tiered products for 2027 Covered California plans.
  • Horizon BCBSNJ. Three-tier “OMNIA” network continues for 2027 with updated hospital placements announced September 2026.
  • Independence Blue Cross. “Keystone HMO Preferred” and “Personal Choice EPO Preferred” tiered designs for Pennsylvania 2027 plans.
  • Independent Health. Three-tier network in Western New York for 2027.
  • Kaiser Permanente. Tiered hospital structures on some 2027 individual plans in Washington, Oregon, and Georgia; Kaiser's California HMO model continues to use a single tier.
  • Oscar. “Classic” and “Secure” tiered PPOs in Florida, Texas, and Tennessee for 2027.
  • Priority Health. “myPriority Tier 1” design in Michigan 2027 plans.
  • Regence BCBS. “Preferred HMO” tier structures in Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Utah for 2027.
  • Several regional provider-owned plans (Geisinger, UPMC, Sanford, Scott & White, Baylor Scott & White, Avera, Sentara, SelectHealth) use preferred-provider tiers that favor the owning health system's facilities.

If your 2027 plan appears above, assume tiered hospital pricing until you confirm otherwise. If your plan is from a carrier not on this list, the plan may still have tiers — tiered network use is spreading quickly and the list grows every year.

The seven-step hospital tier verification protocol

This is the end-to-end process to verify your hospital's 2027 tier placement before December 15. Budget 10 to 20 minutes per plan you are evaluating, and keep screenshots as you go.

1Pull the plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC)

On HealthCare.gov or your state exchange, click through to the plan details page and look for the “Summary of Benefits and Coverage” link (sometimes under a “View All Plan Documents” disclosure). The SBC is a required four-to-six page PDF. Open it and scan the “Common Medical Events” table for a row labeled “If you have a hospital stay” or “Inpatient hospital services.” If the row shows two separate cost-sharing entries (one labeled Tier 1 or similar, one labeled Tier 2 or similar), the plan uses a tiered hospital network. Write the cost-sharing numbers down.

2Open the carrier's provider directory from the plan details page

Click the “View this plan's provider network” link on the HealthCare.gov plan page. This should land you on the carrier's own provider search tool, pre-filtered to the exact 2027 plan you are evaluating. If the pre-filter does not hold (some carrier directories reset the plan filter when you change the search), manually select the exact 2027 plan name again before searching.

Note that many carrier directories call the same plan by a slightly different name than HealthCare.gov. Match on the full legal name and the plan ID that begins with the five-digit carrier HIOS ID.

3Search for your hospital by name and check the tier label

Type the hospital's name (not the system; the specific campus or location) into the directory search. A compliant 2027 directory lists each hospital with an explicit tier label — “Tier 1 Preferred,” “Tier 2 Standard,” “Preferred Provider,” “Center of Excellence,” and similar. If the directory lists the hospital but shows no tier label, the carrier is either (a) using a flat network for that plan or (b) failing to disclose tiers at the shopping stage, which is a violation and worth a complaint to your state insurance department.

Several directories hide tier labels behind a filter rather than displaying them on the main search results. Use the “Filter by tier” or “Preferred providers only” checkbox to confirm whether your hospital appears when the filter is set to Tier 1 only. If it does not, it is Tier 2 or lower.

4Confirm the hospital's affiliated physicians are on the same tier

Tiers apply to the facility, but a Tier 1 hospital with a Tier 2 anesthesiologist, radiologist, or surgical assistant can still produce an unexpected bill. On the same provider directory, search for your admitting physician (or the hospital's main physician group) and verify their tier. On most carrier directories, the physician's tier is shown next to their name. If the physician is on a different tier than the hospital, ask member services whether the physician's tier applies to professional fees or if the facility's tier controls the bill. The No Surprises Act protects you from the biggest surprises here, but tiered cost-sharing on professional fees is legal and common.

5Read the plan's Evidence of Coverage for tier-change language

The Evidence of Coverage (sometimes called the Certificate of Insurance or Benefits Booklet) is the definitive contract. It is 100 to 300 pages and lives on the carrier's site under “Plan Documents” or linked from the SBC. Search the PDF for “tier,” “change in tier,” or “network status.” Look for two things: (a) how much notice the carrier is required to give before moving a hospital between tiers mid-year, and (b) whether Continuity of Care applies if your Tier 1 hospital is moved to Tier 2 during the plan year.

A plan that reserves the right to change tiers “at any time without prior notice” and offers no Continuity of Care language is a meaningful red flag. A plan that guarantees 60-day notice and 90 days of Tier 1 cost-sharing during an active treatment course is well-designed.

6Call the carrier's member services to verbally verify your hospital's tier

Before your enrollment is final, call the carrier's enrollment or shopping line and ask: “For [plan name and ID], is [hospital name and address] a Tier 1 or Tier 2 facility for the 2027 plan year, effective January 1, 2027?” Insist on a specific answer and a case number or reference number. Note the rep's name and the date. If the plan is later found to list the hospital on a different tier than you were told, this record is useful in a complaint to the state insurance department.

Several carriers have a dedicated “benefits advisor” line distinct from general member services that is better-informed about tier placements. Ask for that line specifically.

7Rerun the exercise for a plausible non-hospital scenario

Hospital tiers often apply to outpatient surgery centers and freestanding emergency rooms too, not just inpatient facilities. If you have a scheduled or likely outpatient procedure in 2027 (knee arthroscopy, cataract surgery, colonoscopy, outpatient infusion), look up the specific facility in the same provider directory and verify its tier. The Tier 1-to-Tier 2 gap on outpatient surgery can be $500 to $2,000 per procedure, and these are the costs that most commonly surprise enrollees in January and February.

What to do when your preferred hospital is on Tier 2

Option 1: Pick a different plan where it is Tier 1

If your preferred hospital is Tier 2 on the plan you were going to pick, check whether another 2027 plan from a different carrier lists the same hospital as Tier 1. On many HealthCare.gov marketplaces, a typical enrollee has 8 to 12 Silver plans to choose from, and the same hospital can be Tier 1 on one plan and Tier 2 on another. This is the single highest-impact fix. Pair this comparison with our 2027 ACA plan comparison guide.

Option 2: Accept the plan and budget for Tier 2 exposure

Sometimes the cheapest plan with the best formulary places your hospital on Tier 2. The math still works out if the premium savings exceed the probable extra cost-sharing. Use the Tier 2 OOPM as the upside-cap for your worst-case annual spend on a scheduled admission. For a healthy adult without planned surgery, the premium savings almost always outweigh the Tier 2 exposure. For someone with a scheduled procedure in 2027 or a chronic condition requiring hospital-based care, the Tier 1 plan is usually worth the higher premium.

Option 3: Request a Tier Exception if the plan is otherwise your only option

A Tier Exception (also called a Benefit Tier Exception) is a formal request asking the plan to apply Tier 1 cost-sharing to a Tier 2 facility for medical necessity or network adequacy reasons. Common winning arguments: (a) no Tier 1 hospital provides the specific specialty service within a reasonable travel distance (strong in rural markets); (b) you are mid-treatment at the Tier 2 facility and continuity of care applies; (c) your treating specialist has admitting privileges only at the Tier 2 facility. File the request on the plan's Tier Exception or Network Exception form once your coverage is active; decisions are typically due within 15 business days for non-urgent requests and 72 hours for urgent ones.

Option 4: Negotiate a single-case rate with the hospital's billing office

Some hospitals will accept a plan's Tier 1 negotiated rate for a specific patient and procedure if the plan approves. Call the hospital's financial counseling or billing department before your procedure, explain that you are on a Tier 2 placement, and ask whether they will sign a single-case agreement with your plan at Tier 1 rates. For high-volume elective procedures (cardiac, orthopedic, bariatric), many hospitals will. Our medical bill negotiation guide covers single-case agreements in detail.

Three enrollment mistakes that cost thousands

Mistake 1: Trusting the HealthCare.gov “In-Network” checkmark

The HealthCare.gov plan preview tool shows a green checkmark when a provider is in the plan's network, but the checkmark does not distinguish between tiers. A hospital that is Tier 2 on a tiered plan gets the same visual treatment as a hospital that is Tier 1 on a flat-network plan. Treat the green checkmark as the start of due diligence, not the end.

Mistake 2: Assuming the big-brand academic medical center is Tier 1

Counterintuitively, the largest and most prestigious hospitals in a market are often on Tier 2 because they negotiate higher rates. Community hospitals and regional health systems commonly make Tier 1 because they are cheaper for the carrier. If you want the prestigious hospital in-network at Tier 1 pricing, you may need to pick a different plan entirely — often a higher-premium PPO whose tier design is built around that system.

Mistake 3: Not checking affiliated physician tiers

A hospital on Tier 1 with a staff anesthesiologist on Tier 2 can still generate a tier-sensitive professional fee bill. Verify the tier of the hospital's main physician group, the group that provides anesthesia, and the group that reads imaging. Three separate searches, three separate confirmations.

Special considerations by plan type

HMO plans

Tiered HMOs are growing in 2027. The usual HMO rules apply — primary care referral for specialists, no out-of-network coverage except emergency — plus the added tier structure. On a tiered HMO, your PCP's referral choice can effectively lock you into Tier 2 if the PCP's preferred specialist practices only at a Tier 2 hospital. Choose the PCP with tier awareness.

EPO plans

Tiered EPOs behave like tiered HMOs for the facility network but without the referral requirement. The tier gap can be sharper on an EPO because out-of-network is not an option.

PPO plans

Traditional PPOs with tier structures let you go out-of-network at a higher cost and let you cross tiers inside the network at different cost-sharing. The three-way cost-sharing (Tier 1, Tier 2, out-of-network) can be complex, but PPOs usually give you the most flexibility to pick Tier 1 for scheduled care and accept Tier 2 for convenience.

Catastrophic plans and Bronze plans

These are the plans most likely to use narrow or tiered networks because the carrier prices them aggressively. If you are enrolling in a Bronze plan for 2027, assume tiering and verify before December 15. Catastrophic plans (available to enrollees under 30 or with a hardship exemption) frequently have very narrow Tier 1 lists with large Tier 2 cost gaps.

State-based exchanges with extra tier protections

Several state exchanges go beyond federal rules on hospital tier disclosure and change notices for 2027.

  • Covered California. Freezes hospital tier placement for the entire plan year for all exchange-participating carriers. Tier changes announced at plan launch in October are binding through December 31, 2027.
  • NY State of Health. Requires 60 days' written notice of tier changes and a 90-day Continuity of Care protection for enrollees mid-treatment.
  • Washington Healthplanfinder. Requires tier labels to appear on the shopping page for 2027 plans, not just on the carrier directory — the state exchange's search tool itself filters by tier.
  • Massachusetts Health Connector. Caps the maximum cost-sharing differential between Tier 1 and Tier 2 on exchange-participating plans; plans exceeding the cap cannot be sold on the exchange.
  • Maryland Health Connection. Requires each tiered plan to publish a side-by-side Tier 1 and Tier 2 cost calculator on the carrier's site.

If you are on a state exchange and you see tier information that conflicts with carrier marketing or the SBC, trust the state exchange's disclosure and file a complaint with the state insurance department about the inconsistency.
