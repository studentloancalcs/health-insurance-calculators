How to Check Your 2027 ACA Plan’s Telehealth and Virtual Visit Coverage Before December 15 — Open Enrollment Week 2 Checklist

By HealthCalc Team

Published November 10, 2026

10 min read

Open enrollment is now in its second week, and virtual-visit benefits are one of the shopping variables that has changed the most quietly year to year. Through 2024 and 2025, most Marketplace plans kept the COVID-era flat $0 or low-copay telehealth benefit. For plan years beginning January 1, 2027, the picture is different: cost-sharing has crept up, telehealth networks have narrowed to the enrolling state, and the HSA-compatible "pre-deductible" telehealth safe harbor that makes high-deductible plans usable is set to expire unless Congress acts. If telehealth is a meaningful part of how you actually use health care — therapy, dermatology, primary care follow-ups, GLP-1 refills, chronic care management — the on-paper monthly premium is not enough to compare plans. You need to verify what the plan covers virtually, at what cost, and under what conditions.

This guide walks through a five-minute check you can run on any 2027 Marketplace plan at HealthCare.gov or your state exchange before you lock in a selection by the December 15, 2026 deadline for January 1 coverage. It applies to on-exchange and off-exchange individual plans; most of it also applies to small-group ACA-compliant plans that renew January 1.

Why 2027 is different from 2026

Three things changed between 2026 and 2027 that affect telehealth cost-sharing on individual ACA plans:

  1. The HDHP pre-deductible telehealth safe harbor is sunsetting. Under current law (as of early November 2026), the statutory safe harbor that lets HSA-qualified high-deductible plans cover telehealth before the deductible without disqualifying HSA contributions expires for plan years beginning after December 31, 2026. Congress has extended this provision five times since 2020 and most carriers are pricing 2027 plans as if it will be extended again, but there is no legislation yet. If it is not extended, HDHP enrollees who used $0 virtual visits all year will suddenly be charged the full fair market value of each visit, or the plan loses HSA eligibility for its enrollees.
  2. The MHPAEA 2024 final rule's telehealth provisions are in force. Plans must now document that any difference in telemental health access (prior authorization, network size, referral requirements) has a comparable medical-surgical analog. Several carriers quietly broadened their telemental health networks for 2027 to comply. Others narrowed their medical telehealth networks instead to match the tighter mental-health network — the opposite outcome, same parity math.
  3. State telehealth-specific parity laws are evolving. As of November 2026, 34 states plus DC require payment parity for telehealth, 10 require coverage parity (coverage, not necessarily equal pay), and 6 leave it entirely to the carrier. If you moved or your plan year crossed a state line, the rules changed.
Open-enrollment reality check: Do not assume 2027 telehealth works like 2026 did on the same carrier. Three of the five largest Marketplace carriers (per publicly filed 2027 rate filings) changed at least one telehealth cost-sharing variable between plan years.

The five-minute telehealth verification checklist

Run this checklist on every 2027 plan you are seriously considering, before you click Enroll. You will need the plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC), the formulary (drug list), and the provider directory — all three are linked from the plan details page on HealthCare.gov or your state exchange.

1Pull the SBC and find the "If you need medical attention" rows

The Summary of Benefits and Coverage is a federally standardized document. Scroll to the table labeled Common Medical Event. The rows to look for:

  • Primary care visit to treat an injury or illness — look for a telehealth/virtual visit sub-row or footnote. If there is none, assume the primary-care copay applies.
  • Specialist visit — same.
  • Other practitioner office visit — this is where urgent-care and some therapy-telehealth visits sometimes sit.
  • Mental/behavioral health outpatient services — virtual therapy and psychiatry live here.

If the SBC lists a telehealth-specific amount, use that. If it does not, the plan is applying the regular in-person cost-sharing to virtual visits — which can be fine for a Gold plan with a $20 PCP copay and terrible for a Bronze with a $100 PCP copay or coinsurance after deductible.

2Confirm pre-deductible treatment on HSA-qualified plans

If the plan is HSA-eligible (labeled "HSA" on the Marketplace or with an HDHP minimum deductible of at least $1,700 self-only or $3,400 family for 2027), call the carrier and ask specifically: "If the federal safe harbor for pre-deductible telehealth expires on January 1, 2027, what is your plan's fallback? Will telehealth be charged at full fair market value until the deductible is met?"

You want an answer in writing (secure message, email, or transcribed phone note). Three outcomes are possible: (a) the plan will absorb the cost and treat virtual visits as pre-deductible anyway (uncommon, mostly Kaiser-style integrated plans), (b) the plan will charge the full visit price — often $75–$125 per video visit — until the deductible is met, (c) the plan has a preventive-telehealth sub-benefit that is permissible pre-deductible (immunizations, chronic-care management, tobacco cessation) and bills everything else at full cost. (b) is the most common and the one most likely to bite HSA-plan shoppers who are used to free virtual visits.

3Verify the telehealth provider directory for your state

The plan's provider directory often has a filter labeled Virtual Care, Telehealth, or Video Visit. Apply it, then search for your current providers by name. Two failure modes to watch for:

  • The provider is listed as in-network for in-person care but not for telehealth, because the plan has a separate telehealth contract (sometimes with a third party like Teladoc, Amwell, or MDLive). Your own doctor is still a $30 copay in person and a $75 out-of-network telehealth visit if you connect by video.
  • The provider is only licensed in another state. If you live in Virginia and your therapist is licensed only in DC, your 2027 plan may refuse to pay for a virtual session originating from your Virginia living room, even if the therapist holds an interstate compact license (PSYPACT), unless your plan's documents explicitly honor it.

4Check the formulary for telehealth-prescribed drugs

If you rely on telehealth for refills (ADHD meds, mental-health medications, GLP-1s, hormonal therapy, PrEP), pull the plan's 2027 formulary and verify each drug's tier, prior authorization, step therapy, and quantity limit. Telehealth-only providers cannot work around a formulary exclusion or a PA that requires specialty labs. We maintain a separate guide on matching prescriptions to a 2027 ACA plan formulary that walks through this step.

Controlled substances get an extra check: the DEA's telehealth flexibility for Schedule II–V drugs has been extended through 2027, but the final rule now requires an in-person visit within 6 months of any Schedule II prescription written by telehealth. A virtual-only ADHD-care service that worked in 2026 may force an in-person visit in 2027.

5Verify mental-health parity on telehealth specifically

Compare the SBC line for telehealth medical visits to the SBC line for telehealth mental-health visits. If they differ meaningfully (say, $25 medical / $60 therapy), ask the carrier for the MHPAEA comparative analysis for telehealth. Plans are required to produce it on request as of the 2024 final rule. If they cannot, the differential is probably a parity violation and you have grounds to either pick a different plan or file a complaint after enrolling. Our guide to appealing mental-health parity denials covers the follow-up process.

What the five most common 2027 telehealth patterns look like

We pulled SBCs from a sample of 2027 Marketplace plans at the Silver level in five states. Five patterns of telehealth cost-sharing stood out:

Pattern How telehealth is charged Best for
Flat $0 virtual-first Primary-care video visit: $0. Specialist video: $0 or low copay. Must use the plan's partner (Teladoc, Included Health, Doctor on Demand). People whose main use of health care is routine refills and short consults.
Mirror in-person copay Virtual PCP = in-person PCP copay ($25–$45). Virtual therapy = in-person therapy copay. People who have an established relationship with an in-network provider and want to use them either way.
Virtual-partner only $0 or $10 with the carrier's contracted telehealth partner; your own doctor's video visit counts as out-of-network. People willing to switch to the partner to get free virtual care.
Deductible-first HDHP Full fair-market-value charge until deductible. ~$75–$150 per video visit on the EOB. Only after deductible does coinsurance kick in. Only if you actively prefer the HSA tax benefit and will rarely use telehealth.
Narrow network / state-locked Covered in-network, but only inside the plan's state of issue; out-of-state telehealth is denied even if the provider is in-network for in-person care. People who do not travel or live near a state border.

Three edge cases to confirm before December 15

You live in one state but work in another

Verify that the plan covers telehealth when the patient is physically located in the state of residence AND the state of work. Some plans only cover the state of residence. Virtual visits taken from your office computer may be denied.

You split time between two residences (snowbirds, students)

A policy issued in Minnesota that allows telehealth only when you are physically in Minnesota is not useful if you are in Arizona from December to March. Look for a portability clause or travel benefit in the Schedule of Benefits. Alternatively, a national carrier (Blue Cross with a BlueCard reciprocity note) may cover emergency telehealth anywhere but routine telehealth only in the home state.

You rely on asynchronous "store-and-forward" care (dermatology photos, lab review messages)

Asynchronous telehealth is covered less consistently than synchronous video. If you use a service like Apostrophe, Agency, Hims/Hers, or Nurx and submit to your insurance for reimbursement, confirm the plan's position on asynchronous visits in writing. Many 2027 plans explicitly carve these out of the telehealth benefit.

Putting the numbers together

Once you have the telehealth cost-sharing figure for each plan you are comparing, add it to the total-annual-cost model you are already running. A realistic family-of-three plan comparison for 2027 should include not just premium and deductible, but your expected count of virtual visits times the virtual-visit cost under each plan. Our Plan Cost Calculator accepts a telehealth-visit field explicitly for this reason; running two or three plans side by side will usually surface a $200–$600 annual swing that premium alone hides.

If you qualify for an ACA subsidy, the premium side of that equation is already lower. Run your 2027 estimate through our ACA Subsidy Calculator first so the comparison is of after-subsidy premium plus expected out-of-pocket, not sticker-price premium plus expected out-of-pocket. The math is different and the ranking of plans often flips.

What to do if the best plan on paper has bad telehealth

You have four options, in order of practicality:

  1. Pick a different plan. The Marketplace typically shows 30–80 plans per zip code at the Silver level. The second-cheapest Silver is the subsidy benchmark, but it is not the only one you can pick; another carrier's Silver with better virtual care may cost $20/month more after subsidy and save $300/year in avoided telehealth copays.
  2. Use the carrier's partner rather than your own doctor for routine virtual visits. Reserve your in-person doctor for in-person care. Saves money, keeps continuity for in-person work.
  3. Pay cash for direct-primary-care or a subscription telehealth service for the routine virtual stuff. Combined with a cheap Bronze plan for catastrophic coverage, this can net out cheaper for a healthy single adult. Our DPC + HSA guide walks through the math.
  4. File an MHPAEA complaint after enrolling. Only useful if the telehealth gap is specifically on mental-health visits and you can document a parity violation. Slow process, but has produced cost-sharing refunds in several published settlements.

Common mistakes we are seeing in week two of 2027 OEP

Mistake 1: Trusting the plan name

Plans with names like "Virtual-First," "Digital Direct," "Connect," or "Virtual Choice" are usually (not always) the first pattern above with $0 virtual visits. But a plan called "Digital Advantage" on one carrier may route to a $50 copay partner. The name is marketing. The SBC is binding.

Mistake 2: Assuming the pre-deductible safe harbor will be renewed

It very well may be — it has been extended five times. But as of early November 2026, no 2027 extension has passed. Enroll in an HDHP assuming the worst case (telehealth charged at full fair market value until deductible) and treat an extension as upside. Our broader guide to maximizing your HSA covers the strategy if the safe harbor lapses.

Mistake 3: Letting auto-reenrollment pick the plan for you

Auto-reenrollment keeps your 2026 plan if the carrier still offers it, mapping to the closest 2027 equivalent if it does not. The 2027 equivalent often has different telehealth rules. The 2027 auto-reenrollment handling guide covers how to intercept this.

Bottom line: Telehealth cost-sharing is the 2027 shopping variable that changed the most quietly, and it is the one most likely to surprise you in February after the plan is locked in. Spend the five minutes before December 15 to pull the SBC, filter the provider directory for virtual care, confirm the pre-deductible treatment on any HDHP, and verify parity on telemental-health copays. On an HSA-eligible plan, assume the federal pre-deductible safe harbor will not be extended until Congress acts. On any plan, do not trust the plan name — read the SBC line. The difference between the best and worst 2027 plan on telehealth alone is routinely $300–$600 per year, and the premium quote on the comparison page will not tell you which is which.
