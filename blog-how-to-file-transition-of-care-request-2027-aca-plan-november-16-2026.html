How to File a Transition of Care Request When Switching to a 2027 ACA Plan — The First 60 Days Playbook

By HealthCalc Team

Published November 16, 2026

13 min read

Open enrollment is now midway through its third week, and for roughly 700,000 households whose 2026 insurer is leaving their county, the shopping question has already narrowed to one of two paths. The first path is to pick the cheapest replacement plan and hope your doctor is somewhere in the new network. The second path, the one almost nobody takes because almost nobody knows about it, is to pick the best plan on cost and formulary terms and plan ahead to keep your current doctor in-network anyway for the first 90 days of 2027 through a Transition of Care request.

Cigna is exiting all eleven states in which it offered Marketplace plans. Molina is retrenching from fourteen states to six. UnitedHealthcare is leaving Georgia. Centene's Sunshine State Health Plan is leaving Florida, and Centene is also exiting Delaware and New Hampshire. Baylor Scott & White is leaving Texas. Medica is dropping Iowa, Kansas, and Oklahoma. CareSource is exiting Indiana, West Virginia, and Ohio. PacificSource is exiting Oregon, Idaho, and Montana. Providence Health Plan is winding down Oregon ACA plans. ConnectiCare is leaving the Connecticut exchange. Cox HealthPlans is leaving Missouri. Taro Health is exiting Maine and Oklahoma.

If you are mid-chemotherapy, mid-pregnancy, mid-biologic infusion, mid-radiation, mid-dialysis, mid-rehabilitation, or in the middle of a documented treatment plan on your 2026 plan, a Transition of Care (sometimes called Continuity of Care) request can preserve that provider at in-network cost-sharing for the first 90 days (sometimes longer) of your new 2027 plan — even if the provider is not in your new plan's network at all. This guide walks through exactly how to file one, what documents you need, what to do if denied, and the three big mistakes we see every January.

What Transition of Care actually gets you

Transition of Care is narrower than most people think and broader than the plan's fine print usually suggests. Here is what an approved request does, in plain terms:

  • Reclassifies a specific provider as in-network for you only, for a defined time window, for a defined course of treatment. The provider still bills the new plan, but at in-network cost-sharing to you.
  • Protects you from balance billing at the provider's charge-master rate. If the provider agrees to accept the plan's negotiated rate for this period (most do when asked), you pay your plan's in-network deductible, copay, or coinsurance and nothing more.
  • Preserves authorization for a scheduled course of treatment that was approved under the 2026 plan, such as a chemotherapy regimen, radiation schedule, or infusion series.
  • Does not provide a permanent in-network status. After the Transition of Care window ends (typically 90 days, up to delivery and six weeks postpartum for pregnancy, up to the end of an active cancer course for oncology), you must either transition to an in-network provider or pay full out-of-network costs.
Rule of thumb: If your current treatment has a documented endpoint within the next 90 days, Transition of Care will almost certainly cover you through that endpoint. If it extends longer (biologics with no end date, ongoing cancer surveillance, chronic physical therapy), you will need to either negotiate a longer window with the plan up front or find an in-network provider to take over after day 90.

Who qualifies: the eight condition categories plans accept

Every 2027 ACA plan on HealthCare.gov publishes a Transition of Care policy on its member site. The language varies, but almost all of them accept these eight condition categories:

  1. Active cancer treatment. Chemotherapy, radiation, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or hormone therapy that started under the 2026 plan and has not completed. Surveillance imaging after treatment completion typically does not qualify unless billed as active treatment.
  2. Pregnancy in the second or third trimester. Approval almost always extends through delivery and the standard six-week postpartum period with the current OB, midwife, or maternal-fetal medicine specialist. First-trimester pregnancy usually does not qualify because the plan assumes you can switch to an in-network OB without harm.
  3. Post-surgical or post-transplant follow-up. Scheduled follow-up appointments, physical therapy, and wound care after a surgery performed under the 2026 plan, especially for organ transplants, cardiac procedures, orthopedic reconstruction, or neurosurgery.
  4. Active infusion regimen for a biologic or specialty drug. Infliximab (Remicade and biosimilars), rituximab, ocrelizumab, natalizumab, vedolizumab, and similar biologics on a scheduled infusion calendar.
  5. End-stage renal disease on dialysis. Covered at the dialysis center established on the 2026 plan, typically for the full 90-day window and sometimes longer when the plan and center negotiate directly.
  6. Mental health or substance use disorder treatment plan in progress. Includes intensive outpatient programs (IOPs), partial hospitalization programs (PHPs), residential treatment, and ongoing medication-assisted treatment (MAT) for opioid use disorder.
  7. Terminal illness. Typically defined by the plan as a documented prognosis of six months or less. Coverage usually extends through the terminal phase without a 90-day cap.
  8. Pediatric and developmental therapies. ABA therapy for autism, physical therapy for a pediatric injury, occupational therapy, and speech therapy are increasingly covered under Transition of Care at 2027 ACA plans, particularly on state exchanges in California, New York, Massachusetts, Washington, and Colorado.

Several categories are commonly excluded or require extra documentation: routine primary care visits for a chronic condition without an active treatment plan, elective surgery that has not yet been scheduled, cosmetic procedures, and most second-opinion consultations.

The five-step Transition of Care filing protocol

Work this protocol as soon as you pick your 2027 plan on HealthCare.gov or your state exchange. The earliest practical start is the day after you receive your enrollment confirmation email. The absolute latest is 30 to 90 days after January 1, 2027, depending on the plan.

1Find your new plan's Transition of Care form within 24 hours of enrollment

Once HealthCare.gov confirms your 2027 enrollment, write down the plan's member services number (it is on the confirmation PDF) and go to the plan's main website. Look for a section called "Forms," "New Member," "Member Resources," or "Download Center." The Transition of Care form is usually titled one of: "Transition of Care Request," "Continuity of Care Request," "New Member Continuity Form," or "Out-of-Network Transition Request."

If you cannot find it, call member services the business day after enrollment is confirmed and ask for the Transition of Care form to be emailed to you. Document the time and the rep's name — you may need this later if the plan claims you did not file in time.

2Ask your treating provider for a Transition of Care letter on their letterhead

The letter is almost always the deciding factor. It should include six elements: (a) your full name and date of birth; (b) the exact diagnosis in ICD-10 code form (not plain English); (c) the current treatment plan with specific protocol name, dose, and schedule; (d) the dates when treatment started and when it is expected to end; (e) a sentence stating that a provider change during this active treatment course would cause clinical harm; (f) the provider's NPI number, Medicare/TIN number, and practice address.

Most specialists have a template for this letter because they file them often. If not, send your provider's office a bullet-point summary of what the letter needs to say. Allow three to seven business days for the letter to be drafted. Some providers charge a $25 to $75 administrative fee for records letters. Do not accept a verbal confirmation or a prescription pad note. Insurers routinely reject non-letterhead or non-signed documents.

3Compile and submit the complete packet with a clear timeline

A compliant packet includes: the completed Transition of Care form, the provider letter from step 2, your plan ID or Marketplace application ID, and copies of any prior-authorization approvals your 2026 plan issued for this treatment. If you have an active infusion schedule or chemotherapy calendar, include it.

Submit by whichever method the plan prefers in this priority order: (1) the plan's member portal upload tool, which creates a timestamped receipt; (2) fax to the number on the Transition of Care form, saving the fax confirmation page; (3) certified mail with tracking. Email is a last resort and often not accepted. Note the exact submission date — the 10-business-day decision clock starts from the plan's receipt.

4Confirm receipt and follow up in writing within seven days

Three business days after submission, call member services and ask for a case number. Get the name and extension of the Transition of Care coordinator assigned to your case. Email the coordinator a one-paragraph confirmation of what you submitted and when.

If seven calendar days pass with no case number, call again and escalate. Say explicitly: "I filed a Transition of Care request on [date] and have not received a case number. I need to speak with a supervisor." Document everything. For most 2027 plans, the clock to issue a decision is 15 calendar days for non-urgent requests and 72 hours for urgent ones, measured from the plan's receipt.

5Negotiate directly with your provider while you wait

Transition of Care approval depends partly on your provider agreeing to accept the new plan's negotiated rate. Call your provider's billing office and ask explicitly: "If I am granted Transition of Care, will you accept [new plan name]'s negotiated rate for the next 90 days without balance billing me?" Most established providers will say yes for an existing patient. A small number (concierge practices, out-of-network specialists in rural markets) may decline; if so, Transition of Care will not help you and you will need to find a willing provider.

Ask your provider to sign a one-page single-case agreement with the plan if the plan requires it — this is routine and accelerates approval. A few plans only approve Transition of Care if the provider has already signed the single-case agreement before the request is reviewed.

What to do if Transition of Care is denied

Step A: File an internal appeal within 60 days

The denial letter will name an appeals address and a deadline. Appeal in writing within that window. The appeal should add material that was not in the original submission, such as: a letter from a second treating provider confirming medical necessity for continuity, peer-reviewed literature showing treatment disruption harm, documentation that no in-network provider offers the specific protocol or has availability within 30 miles or a reasonable travel distance (a strong argument in rural counties). Non-urgent appeal decisions are due within 30 days; urgent appeals, 72 hours.

Step B: Request External Review

If the internal appeal is denied, request External Review through an Independent Review Organization (IRO). This is free and the decision is legally binding on the plan. The plan will include External Review instructions with the final internal denial. Deadlines vary by state but are typically 4 months from the internal denial. IROs overturn plan decisions roughly 25 to 40 percent of the time when the enrollee has documented mid-treatment care, according to state insurance department reports.

Step C: Negotiate a single-case agreement directly

Many out-of-network providers will sign a single-case agreement with a plan for one specific patient. This is especially common for cancer centers, high-volume infusion centers, and specialty surgical practices. Have your provider's billing office call the plan's provider network team and request a single-case agreement. If both sides agree, you pay in-network cost-sharing without ever filing Transition of Care. Our out-of-network exception guide covers the single-case agreement in detail.

Step D: File a complaint with your state insurance commissioner

Every state has an insurance department that accepts consumer complaints about health plan denials. State regulators can investigate, impose sanctions, and sometimes order coverage even after an external review denial, particularly when there is a pattern of unsupported denials by the carrier. Search "[your state] insurance commissioner health plan complaint" for the right form. California, New York, Washington, Oregon, and Massachusetts have especially active health plan oversight.

Three mistakes we see every January

Mistake 1: Waiting until the first denied claim to file

The most common mistake is the one with the worst consequences. Patients receive a January or February out-of-network claim denial and only then discover Transition of Care exists. By that point, the filing window has often closed, the provider has already billed at charge-master rates, and the patient is facing a five- or six-figure bill. File Transition of Care before January 1, 2027, if at all possible. If the plan's enrollment system is not ready to accept it, call member services the business day after your Marketplace confirmation email and ask whether the Transition of Care team can accept an early filing.

Mistake 2: Confusing Transition of Care with a formulary tier exception

Transition of Care is about keeping an out-of-network provider in-network for you. A formulary exception is about keeping an out-of-formulary or higher-tier drug affordable. They are different processes with different forms and different criteria. If your 2027 plan's formulary places your specialty drug on tier 5 but your 2026 provider is in-network, file a formulary exception only. If your provider is out-of-network but your drug is on the formulary, file Transition of Care only. If both are out of alignment, file both separately. Our specialty drug tier guide covers the formulary exception process in detail.

Mistake 3: Letting the provider control the timeline

Specialist offices are busy and a records letter is low priority. If you ask for it on December 20 and have not received it by December 30, you will be one of thousands of patients contacting the office the first week of January. Ask for the letter in the first week after your enrollment confirmation, not the last. Follow up every three business days. If the office cannot produce the letter within seven business days, ask whether the office manager can send a bullet-point summary on letterhead instead, which is almost always acceptable to the plan while the full letter is drafted.

Special cases: HMO, EPO, and state-based exchanges

HMO plans

HMO plans typically require you to designate a primary care provider (PCP) and get referrals for specialists. A Transition of Care request on an HMO can cover both the specialist and the specialist's referral authority, but the plan will usually require you to also pick an in-network PCP during the Transition of Care window. Pick one quickly, even if you do not intend to see them, to prevent the request from being delayed on administrative grounds.

EPO plans

EPO plans do not cover any out-of-network care except emergencies. Transition of Care is especially important on an EPO because there is no out-of-network cost-sharing fallback — without approval, you pay 100% of billed charges. EPO Transition of Care tends to be slightly stricter on documentation and slightly faster on decision timelines because the alternative is so severe for the enrollee.

State-based exchanges (Covered California, NY State of Health, MNsure, Access Health CT, Washington Healthplanfinder)

Several state exchanges impose stronger continuity-of-care requirements than federal rules. Covered California requires 12 months of Transition of Care coverage for pregnancy and up to 12 months for cancer treatment on all exchange-participating carriers. NY State of Health requires 60 days for most conditions and up to 90 for cancer and pregnancy. Massachusetts Health Connector requires 30 days minimum with extensions for active treatment. If you are on a state-based exchange, read the exchange's Continuity of Care page before relying on the carrier's default policy.

Bottom line: For the roughly 700,000 households picking a new 2027 ACA plan after an insurer exit, Transition of Care is the single most valuable piece of paperwork you can file in the first 30 days of the new plan. Start the day after HealthCare.gov confirms your 2027 enrollment: find the form, request the provider letter, compile the packet, submit by portal or fax, and confirm receipt in writing. Keep your provider in the loop on the plan's negotiated rate so a single-case agreement is ready if needed. A one-page letter and a correctly filed form can be the difference between a disrupted treatment course and a seamless 90-day bridge to your new plan's network.
