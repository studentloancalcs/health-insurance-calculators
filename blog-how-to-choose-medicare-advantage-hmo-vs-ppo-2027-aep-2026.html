How to Choose Between a Medicare Advantage HMO and PPO Plan for 2027: The AEP 2026 Network, Cost, and Access Playbook

By HealthCalc Team

Published October 20, 2026

11 min read

For twenty years the Medicare Advantage HMO-vs-PPO decision has been described as a tradeoff between cost and flexibility: HMOs cost less, PPOs let you go out-of-network. That framing is still broadly correct, but the 2027 plan year is pushing the two product types farther apart than they have been in a decade. UnitedHealthcare, which covers roughly 29 percent of Medicare Advantage enrollment, is cutting its 2027 individual MA plan count from 12,421 to 11,734 and openly “pulling back hardest from broker-sold PPOs,” according to Leerink Partners analyst Whit Mayo. Centene is reducing its county footprint by 20 percent. Aetna and Elevance Health are each contracting by 5 percent. Molina is exiting individual MA entirely.

At the same time, the average Medicare Advantage out-of-pocket maximum is rising 9.1 percent for 2027, with Centene up 16.1 percent and Clover Health up 28.5 percent, per Fierce Healthcare's analysis of 2027 landscape files. The federal ceilings — $9,350 in-network, $14,000 combined in- and out-of-network — apply equally to HMOs and PPOs, but the averages are drifting toward the ceiling faster on the PPO side.

If you are shopping during AEP 2026 (October 15 through December 7, 2026) for a plan that starts January 1, 2027, the HMO-vs-PPO decision is not a philosophical one. It is a question of whether a specific set of doctors, hospitals, drugs, and expected utilization is better served by the restricted-but-cheaper network or by the broader-but-pricier one. This guide walks through the four questions that decide it.

Two things in that table do most of the explanatory work. First, HMO out-of-pocket maximums average almost $2,000 less than PPO in-network maximums — and the PPO combined maximum is a different number altogether, roughly double. Second, the physician access advertised on a PPO’s brochure is softer than it looks: an out-of-network Medicare Advantage patient can be declined by any provider, because providers are not required to accept your plan’s payment terms. UnitedHealthcare reports its HMO enrollees spent $175 less per year in out-of-pocket costs on average than its PPO enrollees. The gap is not large if you never leave the network. It is large if you do.

Question 1 — Are Your Doctors In-Network for 2027?

Not for 2026. For 2027. This is the single most important question of AEP 2026, and it is the one most retirees skip because the 2026 directory felt stable. For 2027 it will not be. UnitedHealthcare is restructuring its PPO offerings. Aetna, Humana, and Elevance are each renegotiating hospital contracts, and in some markets dropping large systems. The 2027 directory is the one that matters.

How to check

  1. Open the plan’s 2027 provider directory on medicare.gov’s Plan Finder. Use the 2027 plan year tab, not the current year.
  2. Search each of your doctors by name and NPI. For hospitals, confirm not just the system name but the specific campus and department. A plan that lists “Kaiser” in the directory may exclude a specific Kaiser specialty clinic.
  3. Call the office. The directory is only as current as the last update; some plans publish stale directories into mid-November. The front desk knows whether they are seeing 2027 patients on that plan.
  4. If you are considering an HMO, confirm the primary care physician who will coordinate your care accepts the plan and is accepting new patients. HMO PCP panels close faster than specialist panels.
The “will accept Medicare” trap. A provider accepting Medicare does not mean they accept your specific Medicare Advantage plan. Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage are different payers with different contracts. Always ask: “Do you accept [plan name] for 2027?”

Question 2 — How Often Will You Actually Go Out-of-Network?

A PPO’s out-of-network benefit is only worth paying for if you use it. The honest version of this question is: in the last 24 months, how many of your medical visits were to providers you would struggle to replace in a strong local HMO network?

For most retirees the answer is zero to one. Primary care, labs, imaging, cardiology, orthopedics, and routine specialty care are typically well-covered inside large HMO networks in metropolitan counties. The out-of-network use cases that actually justify a PPO are narrower:

  • Snowbirds who split the year across two states and want in-network-level coverage in both. Note: some HMOs offer a “travel benefit” or convert to passport coverage for part of the year; check the Evidence of Coverage before assuming an HMO fails here.
  • Patients with a specific out-of-network specialist whose expertise is not available in the HMO’s network. If the physician is in the PPO’s network, the PPO just acts as a wider HMO. If the physician is out-of-network for both plans, the PPO is the only option that pays anything.
  • Rural beneficiaries whose nearest in-network tertiary care center is more than an hour away. A PPO’s out-of-network coverage can keep the local hospital usable even if it is not contracted.
  • Patients with an active clinical trial or specialty treatment in another state.

If none of those describe you, the PPO’s premium bump and higher out-of-pocket maximum are paying for an option you will not use. The HMO is almost certainly the better value.

Question 3 — What Is Your Realistic Worst-Case Year?

The out-of-pocket maximum is where the HMO-vs-PPO gap bites. Imagine two bad years:

Bad year, in-network only. Hip replacement, four weeks of physical therapy, two cardiology admissions. All in-network. On an HMO with a $4,636 in-network cap, your worst-case medical cost sharing is $4,636. On a PPO with a $6,592 in-network cap, your worst-case is $6,592. Difference: $1,956.

Bad year, mixed. You travel for a specialty procedure out of state at an out-of-network academic center. Charges hit $120,000. On an HMO, non-emergency out-of-network care is not covered at all; you pay the full negotiated amount. On a PPO, you are protected to the combined cap of $14,000. The PPO is dramatically better here.

The question is whether the second scenario is realistic for you. If it is — a planned complex surgery, an active cancer treatment, or a chronic condition that requires an out-of-area specialist — the PPO premium and higher in-network cap are reasonable prices for the out-of-network protection. If the scenario is remote, the premium is insurance against an event that is unlikely to happen.

The one math shortcut that works: multiply the HMO/PPO premium difference by 12. Add the HMO/PPO in-network OOP max difference. The resulting number is the premium you are paying for out-of-network protection in a bad year. If that number is less than your estimated out-of-network cost in a realistic worst case, the PPO is worth it. Otherwise the HMO wins.

Question 4 — What Does the 2027 ANOC Say About Your Current Plan?

If you are already in a Medicare Advantage plan, the Annual Notice of Change your plan mailed by September 30, 2026 is the document that frames your 2027 decision. The lines that matter:

  • Plan type. Confirm it is still an HMO, HMO-POS, or PPO. A plan can change its product category — some carriers are converting PPOs to HMOs for 2027 to shrink networks.
  • In-network out-of-pocket maximum. Compare the 2026 and 2027 columns. A $500+ increase without a benefit improvement elsewhere is a reason to shop.
  • Combined out-of-pocket maximum (PPOs only). Check whether the combined cap moved toward the $14,000 ceiling.
  • Prior authorization list. The ANOC lists any new services that require prior authorization in 2027. CMS-4201-F clarified prior authorization standards starting in 2026; the services your plan requires can change year to year.
  • Specialist copay changes. A $5 increase on specialist visits is $60 to $150 a year depending on utilization.
  • Supplemental benefit changes. Dental, vision, hearing, OTC. Many plans are reducing these benefits for 2027 even as they lower premiums.
  • Non-renewal statement. If your plan is non-renewing, the ANOC and a separate non-renewal letter say so in bold. A Special Enrollment Period opens that extends through the end of February 2027 and preserves Medigap guaranteed-issue rights for most beneficiaries.

If any of those lines are moving in the wrong direction, the AEP is the time to shop — and the HMO-vs-PPO decision is embedded in that shopping. A PPO whose in-network cap jumped $800 and whose provider directory lost two of your doctors has effectively become a worse plan, and switching to an HMO that still has your doctors in-network may be the easiest path to lower 2027 cost sharing.

The Prior Authorization Reality Check

A common assumption is that PPOs have fewer prior authorization hurdles than HMOs. The data say otherwise. KFF's 2026 analysis found 99 percent of all Medicare Advantage enrollees — HMO and PPO alike — are in plans requiring prior authorization for at least some services. The share of enrollees subject to prior authorization by service type:

  • Acute inpatient hospital stays: 97 percent
  • Skilled nursing facility stays: 95 percent
  • Part B drugs: 94 percent
  • Psychiatric inpatient stays: 93 percent
  • Home health services: 90 percent
  • Preventive services: 6 percent

In 2024, Medicare Advantage insurers received almost 53 million prior authorization requests and denied 4.1 million, roughly 8 percent, per KFF’s compilation of CMS data. Prior authorization is a Medicare Advantage feature, not an HMO one. If avoiding prior authorization is a priority, Original Medicare plus a Medigap plan and a standalone Part D plan is the only configuration that eliminates it for most services. Within Medicare Advantage, pick on network and cost; prior authorization will be present either way.

Who Each Plan Type Fits Best

The HMO (or HMO-POS) fits you if

  • Your doctors and preferred hospital are in a strong local network
  • You rarely travel out of your home region for care
  • You are willing to work through a primary care physician
  • You value the lower out-of-pocket maximum and the richer supplemental benefits HMOs often carry
  • Your worst realistic year keeps you inside your market’s network

The PPO fits you if

  • You split the year between two states or regions
  • You have an out-of-state specialist you want preserved access to
  • You are in or likely to enter an active treatment that may require an out-of-network academic center
  • You live in a rural market where the HMO network is thin
  • You value the flexibility to self-refer and are willing to pay a higher in-network maximum for it

Neither fits you if

  • You want zero prior authorization — consider Original Medicare + Medigap + Part D
  • You expect care in multiple states but want the strongest supplemental benefits — the right PPO may still outperform Original Medicare + Medigap on dental, vision, and hearing, so compare the full package rather than the plan category alone
  • You qualify for a Dual Eligible Special Needs Plan (D-SNP) or Chronic Condition SNP (C-SNP) — those products target your situation better than a generic HMO or PPO

The Four-Step AEP Checklist

  1. Read your 2027 ANOC. Pay attention to the plan type, in-network OOP max, prior authorization list, and non-renewal statement. If your plan is non-renewing, confirm your SEP dates and Medigap guaranteed-issue rights.
  2. Build your medicare.gov Plan Finder account for 2027. Load every drug, every pharmacy, every doctor. Pull the 2027 HMO list and the 2027 PPO list in your ZIP code.
  3. Run the four questions. Doctors in-network for 2027? Out-of-network use realistic? Worst-case year math? ANOC moving in the right direction? If the first three favor the HMO, the fourth usually confirms it.
  4. Call SHIP (1-877-839-2675) for a free, unbiased second opinion. State Health Insurance Assistance Program counselors do not sell insurance and have no commission on any plan. One hour with a SHIP volunteer is the single best AEP investment most beneficiaries make.

If you miss December 7, you have two safety valves. The Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period (MA OEP) runs January 1 through March 31, 2027 and allows one switch between MA plans or from MA back to Original Medicare. A 5-star Special Enrollment Period lets you switch into any 5-star plan in your county at almost any time of year.

The Bottom Line

The HMO-vs-PPO decision is really a bet on your own healthcare pattern. If your care stays local and your doctors are in-network, the HMO wins on average by roughly $175 to $2,000 a year, carries a lower out-of-pocket maximum, and often bundles richer dental and vision benefits. If you travel, split the year, or anticipate out-of-network specialty care, the PPO’s premium is reasonable insurance.

The 2027 wrinkle is that PPO networks are being actively narrowed. UnitedHealthcare’s pullback is the loudest, but it is not alone. If you are already in a PPO, assume the 2027 network is smaller than the 2026 one until the 2027 provider directory proves otherwise. If you are already in an HMO, check that the network still covers your care; some HMO networks are growing, but not all. The ANOC and the Plan Finder together, read side by side, answer in about an hour what carrier marketing cannot.
