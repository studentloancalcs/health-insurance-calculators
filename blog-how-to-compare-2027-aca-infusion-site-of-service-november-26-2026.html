How to Compare 2027 ACA Plans' Infusion Site-of-Service Rules Before December 15, 2026 — The Hospital-Outpatient vs. Free-Standing Suite Trap

By HealthCalc Team

Published November 26, 2026

13 min read

Two patients sit in adjacent recliners at the same infusion center, run by the same nurses, receiving the same biologic drug from the same pharmacy stock. One patient’s 2027 ACA plan sends a bill for $75. The other patient’s bill is $4,218. The drug cost the plan $11,400 either way. Nothing changed clinically. What changed is the place of service code the infusion center put on each patient’s claim — and whether each patient’s 2027 plan reads that code as an in-office specialist visit (cheap) or as a hospital outpatient department visit (expensive).

This is the site-of-service differential, and for anyone on a biologic, chemotherapy, IVIG, Remicade, Ocrevus, Entyvio, Benlysta, or any other high-cost infused drug, it is the single largest cost variable on a 2027 ACA plan. It dwarfs the drug tier, the deductible, and the out-of-pocket maximum — because for a $14,000-per-infusion drug given every six weeks, the difference between a flat copay and a coinsurance percentage repeats every six weeks until you hit the out-of-pocket max, and then repeats next January 1 when the max resets.

This is the pre-Dec-15 window-shopping checklist that infusion patients almost never get told about by brokers or by the marketplace: what the site-of-service differential is, how to find it in a 2027 Schedule of Benefits that does not use that phrase anywhere, how to tell whether your current infusion center bills as a hospital or a free-standing site, how to verify a 2027 plan’s treatment of that site before you enroll, and how to request site-of-care prior authorization so you are routed to the cheaper setting from day one.

What the site-of-service differential actually is

Health insurance claims carry a two-digit “place of service” (POS) code that tells the payer where the service happened. For infusions, five codes do most of the work:

POS code What it means Typical 2027 ACA plan cost-share
11 — Office Physician’s own office infusion room, independent of a hospital Specialist copay (often $40–$100) or “drugs administered in office” cost-share
49 — Independent clinic / 20 — Urgent care Free-standing infusion clinic, not hospital-owned Specialist or office-visit tier; similar to POS 11
22 — On-campus outpatient hospital Infusion suite physically inside or adjacent to a hospital building Outpatient hospital cost-share (often 20%–40% coinsurance after deductible, plus facility fee)
19 — Off-campus outpatient hospital Hospital-owned clinic across town, billed as a hospital department Usually outpatient hospital cost-share; some 2027 plans apply site-neutral pricing if claim carries modifier PN
24 — Ambulatory surgical center Free-standing surgical center that also provides infusions Specialist or outpatient surgery tier; varies by plan

Two infusion sites that look identical to a patient — recliners, IV poles, a nurse, a drug — can carry different POS codes because of their ownership and licensure, not because of what happens during the visit. A hospital system that acquires a community oncology practice often re-licenses the practice as a hospital outpatient department (an HOPD). Nothing about the clinical setting changes. Everything about the billing changes.

Quick framing. Where the infusion happens does not change what the nurse does. It changes which cost-share line on your 2027 Schedule of Benefits applies. The drug is the same. The claim path is different. The bill is different.

Why the differential is usually four figures per infusion

Hospital outpatient departments bill under the facility’s outpatient prospective payment system (OPPS) rate, which is historically 2–3x the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule rate for the same drug administration code. Commercial ACA plans largely mirror that structure: they apply the outpatient hospital cost-share to HOPD claims and the specialist or in-office drug cost-share to POS 11, 49, or 24 claims.

For a typical high-cost infused biologic with a plan allowed amount of $12,000 per dose and an administration code (CPT 96365, 96413, etc.) of $600, a 2027 Silver ACA plan might apply:

  • Free-standing / POS 11 scenario: $75 specialist copay for the visit, drug covered at zero cost-share because it is a medical-benefit injectable with no coinsurance. Total patient cost per infusion: $75.
  • Hospital outpatient / POS 22 scenario: $2,500 medical deductible remaining, then 30% coinsurance on the $12,600 allowed amount, capped by the plan’s $9,200 individual out-of-pocket maximum. First infusion of the year: $2,500 deductible + 30% of $10,100 = $5,530. Subsequent infusions at 30% coinsurance until OOP max is hit, then $0.

On a patient receiving infusions every six weeks (nine per year), the free-standing path totals $675 for the year. The HOPD path totals the full $9,200 OOP max. The spread is $8,525 per year, every year the patient stays on the medication, for a service identical in every clinical respect.

This is not theoretical. CMS’s own 2027 OPPS/ASC proposed rule and the Medicare site-neutral payment expansion that took effect in 2026 both exist precisely because the differential has become unsustainable for Medicare; commercial ACA plans are following, slowly, with their own 2027 site-of-service rules. The slow pace is why you still need to check your specific plan before December 15.

How to read the 2027 Schedule of Benefits for the differential

The Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC) for a 2027 ACA plan rarely uses the phrase “site of service.” You have to infer the differential from three line items. Open each 2027 plan’s SBC and locate:

Line 1: “Specialty drugs administered in office”

This is the cost-share for infused or injected specialty drugs when billed under POS 11 (physician office). Look for phrases like “drugs administered in office,” “physician-administered specialty drugs,” “Part-B-like drugs,” “J-code drugs,” or “Tier 4/Tier 5 drugs administered by provider.” A typical 2027 value is a flat copay ($30–$100) or a modest coinsurance (10%–20% with a per-visit cap).

Line 2: “Outpatient hospital services” or “Outpatient facility”

This is the cost-share that applies when the exact same drug is billed under POS 22 (on-campus hospital outpatient) or sometimes POS 19 (off-campus HOPD). A typical 2027 value is 20%–40% coinsurance after the medical deductible, with no per-visit cap.

Line 3: “Preferred infusion site” or “Site of care” (newer, 2027 plans)

Some 2027 ACA issuers have added a third tier: a reduced cost-share that applies only at a plan-designated “preferred infusion site” (sometimes called a Center of Excellence or Infusion Center of Excellence). Look for phrases like “preferred site,” “site of care program,” “home infusion program,” or “infusion COE.” If this line exists, it is usually the cheapest option — often a $0–$50 copay for the entire infusion — and routing yourself to a preferred site is the single most valuable move an infusion patient can make on a 2027 ACA plan.

If the SBC does not show a line for in-office specialty drugs or has them under “see EOC”: the SBC is not enough. Download the full Evidence of Coverage (EOC) PDF for that 2027 plan and search it for “infusion,” “place of service,” “site of care,” and “facility fee.” The site-of-service rules almost always live in the EOC’s medical policy section, not in the plan comparison grids on healthcare.gov.

How to find out how your current infusion center bills

Before you can shop 2027 plans intelligently, you need to know which site code your infusion center uses today. The clinical setting is not a reliable indicator. A free-standing-looking suite in a strip mall can still bill as POS 22 if the hospital system that owns it has licensed it as a provider-based department. Three checks:

Check 1: Ask the billing department, not the front desk

Front-desk staff often do not know. The billing department does. Call the main number for the infusion center and ask to be transferred to billing. Say: “I am on a commercial insurance plan. For infusion claims, do you bill under place of service 11, 22, 19, or 24?” A willing biller will answer in one sentence. If they cannot or will not tell you, ask them to show you a redacted sample claim or EOB for a commercial patient.

Check 2: Ask about modifier PO or PN

If the billing department tells you POS 19 or POS 22, follow up with: “Does the claim carry modifier PO or modifier PN?” Modifier PO identifies a grandfathered off-campus HOPD paid at the full OPPS rate. Modifier PN identifies a non-grandfathered off-campus HOPD paid at the Medicare site-neutral rate, which some 2027 ACA plans now mirror. A PN site billed to a plan that applies site-neutral pricing commercially will carry a lower cost-share than a PO site, even though both are technically hospital outpatient departments.

Check 3: Verify via your most recent EOB

Pull up an explanation of benefits (EOB) from your last infusion on your current plan. The EOB lists the provider, the CPT codes billed, and usually the place of service code. If POS 22 appears, the claim was billed as on-campus outpatient. If the line shows “outpatient hospital” language or a facility fee separate from the drug, the claim was billed as HOPD. If you see only a physician-services line with a specialist copay applied, the claim was billed as POS 11 or POS 49. Our EOB reading guide walks through exactly where these codes appear.

If the answer is POS 22 (on-campus HOPD) or POS 19/PO (grandfathered off-campus HOPD), you have a known high-cost site, and the question becomes whether any 2027 plan treats that site more favorably, and whether a free-standing alternative exists in-network.

The pre-Dec-15 verification protocol for infusion patients

1List every infusion-eligible site within 30 miles. Include your current site, every free-standing infusion suite you can find on Google Maps or on your current insurer’s facility directory, every physician office that offers in-office infusions for your drug, and any home-infusion provider that services your ZIP code. Note the full legal name and address of each.

2For each candidate 2027 plan, check if each site is in-network. Open the 2027 provider directory for each plan on healthcare.gov window-shopping or on the issuer’s plan-finder page. Search by the facility’s legal name, its NPI if you can find it on CMS’s NPI registry, and its address. A site that does not appear in the 2027 directory is out-of-network for 2027 even if it was in-network for 2026.

3Download each 2027 plan’s SBC and EOC. Record the in-office specialty drug cost-share, the outpatient hospital cost-share, and any preferred-site line. If the EOC mentions a “site of care program” or a “home infusion program,” note the plan’s contact number for enrollment in that program.

4Call each finalist plan’s member services with your specific drug. Give them the J-code (J1745 for Remicade, J2350 for Ocrevus, J1569 for IVIG Gammagard, etc.) and the names of the two or three sites you would use. Ask: “For a 2027 enrollee receiving J[code] every [frequency] at [site name], what will the per-infusion patient cost-share be?” Get the member services rep’s name and a reference number. Repeat for each site.

5Project the annual out-of-pocket cost for each plan + site combination. Multiply the per-infusion cost-share by the number of infusions per year, then cap at the plan’s individual out-of-pocket maximum. The plan that minimizes your total projected cost, including the premium, is the right 2027 plan for your clinical situation. Use the Plan Cost Calculator to compare the totals side-by-side.

Site-of-care prior authorization: steering yourself to the cheaper site

Many 2027 commercial ACA plans now require a separate “site of care” or “site of service” prior authorization in addition to the usual drug prior authorization. This can actually work in your favor: the authorization steers covered care to the lowest-cost appropriate site, which is almost always the free-standing or physician-office site.

If your 2027 plan has a site-of-care authorization requirement, ask your prescribing physician to submit both authorizations at the same time, and to specify the preferred free-standing site by name and address on the authorization form. The authorization decision typically lists an “approved site” by NPI. Keep that approval letter — when you call to schedule the infusion, give the scheduler the approved site’s NPI and ask them to verify the site matches before booking the appointment.

If the plan’s site-of-care policy denies coverage at the hospital outpatient department, you can appeal based on clinical necessity (first dose of a high-risk biologic, prior infusion reaction requiring crash cart, pediatric patient, documented need for anesthesia or sedation, lack of appropriate free-standing site within a reasonable distance). Our prior authorization appeal guide walks through the clinical-necessity framework.

If your doctor refuses to submit a site-of-care authorization: you can submit one yourself through the plan’s member portal in most cases. You will need the physician’s name and NPI, the drug and J-code, the diagnosis code (ICD-10), the frequency, and the requested site’s name and NPI. Attach a letter asking for the site-of-care authorization, citing the EOC section on site-of-care programs by name.

Three enrollment mistakes that cost infusion patients thousands in 2027

Mistake 1: Picking the plan with the lowest premium and the smallest deductible, ignoring the outpatient-hospital coinsurance

A $0 deductible Silver plan with $75 specialty drug copay sounds like the cheapest choice until you read the outpatient hospital line and see 40% coinsurance. If your infusion site bills as HOPD, that 40% is your cost per infusion, up to the OOP max — the $0 deductible is irrelevant because the deductible applies to hospital services too. Always compare by the outpatient-hospital coinsurance when you are an infusion patient, not by the deductible.

Mistake 2: Assuming “in-network” means the cost-share will be the same at every in-network site

In-network only means the plan has a contract with the site and will not balance-bill you above the allowed amount. It says nothing about which cost-share tier applies. An in-network HOPD charges the HOPD cost-share. An in-network free-standing site charges the free-standing cost-share. Both are “in-network;” the bills are not comparable.

Mistake 3: Not checking continuity-of-care rules when switching plans

If you are mid-course on a biologic and switching 2027 plans, the new plan may require a fresh prior authorization, a fresh site-of-care authorization, and a transition-of-care request to maintain your current infusion schedule without a gap. File the transition-of-care request within the new plan’s stated window (typically 30 to 90 days from the effective date) and attach the current plan’s approval letter. Our transition-of-care request guide covers the paperwork step by step.

When the only in-network site is an HOPD

In many regions, especially smaller metros and rural areas, hospital systems have consolidated infusion services into HOPDs and closed free-standing competitors. If no free-standing in-network site exists within a reasonable distance, you have a few options for 2027:

  • Request a physician-office infusion. If the prescribing physician’s office has an infusion room (even a small one), the office can usually bill as POS 11 at the specialist copay rate. Ask the office manager whether they infuse your specific drug, and if not, whether they are willing to.
  • Enroll in a plan’s home infusion program. Several 2027 ACA plans offer home infusion by a plan-contracted nursing service for stable patients on established biologics. This is typically billed at the free-standing rate or lower (often $0 patient cost). Ask member services whether your drug is eligible for home infusion.
  • Negotiate an in-network exception for an out-of-network free-standing site. If the nearest free-standing site is out-of-network, you can request an exception based on network inadequacy. Document that no in-network free-standing option exists within the plan’s stated access standard (typically 30 miles or 30 minutes for routine care; sometimes 60 for specialty). Our out-of-network exception guide covers the request.
  • Choose a 2027 plan with a lower outpatient hospital coinsurance. If an HOPD is your only realistic site, the lowest-cost plan is the one with the lowest outpatient hospital coinsurance and the lowest OOP maximum, regardless of the specialty drug line.
