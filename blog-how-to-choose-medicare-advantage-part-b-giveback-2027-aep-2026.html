How to Choose a Medicare Advantage Plan with a Part B Giveback for 2027: What’s Changing During AEP 2026

By HealthCalc Team

Published October 18, 2026

10 min read

The Medicare Part B giveback has quietly become one of the most-marketed benefits in Medicare Advantage. In 2018, only about 4.3 percent of Medicare Advantage plans offered one. By 2024, nearly 19 percent did, and 3.4 million enrollees were receiving an average of $77 per month in Part B premium reductions — roughly $924 a year back in their pockets, delivered as a smaller deduction from each Social Security check. For a retiree on a fixed income, that is real money.

Then 2027 arrived in planning rooms. In a 2026 HealthScape Advisors survey of 35 Medicare Advantage plan leaders, nearly 70 percent said their 2027 benefit packages would be less rich than their 2026 packages. Part B givebacks are at the top of the cut list, alongside major dental benefits and specialist copays. At the same time, CMS projects the national average Medicare Advantage premium will fall from $14.37 to about $12 per month, a 16.5 percent drop. The headline is friendly. The small print, for anyone whose 2026 plan includes a meaningful giveback, is not.

This guide walks through the giveback the way an unbiased SHIP counselor would: what it actually is, who can use it, how to spot it on the Medicare Plan Finder during the Annual Enrollment Period (AEP, October 15 through December 7, 2026), the three tradeoffs that are easy to miss, and the one line in your 2027 Annual Notice of Change (ANOC) that tells you whether your current giveback survives.

Quick snapshot for AEP 2026 (for 2027 coverage):
  • 2026 standard Part B premium: $202.90/month (the giveback is applied against this)
  • AEP window: October 15, 2026 – December 7, 2026
  • Average 2024 giveback: $77/month across 3.4 million enrollees
  • Projected 2027 MA average premium: $12/month (down 16.5%)
  • Share of plan executives expecting leaner 2027 benefits: ~70%

What the Part B Giveback Actually Is

Despite the marketing, the Part B giveback is not a check, a flex card, or an over-the-counter allowance. It is a reduction of the standard Medicare Part B premium, applied through one of the two channels Medicare uses to collect that premium.

If your Part B premium is deducted from your Social Security check — which it is for the vast majority of beneficiaries — the amount deducted shrinks by the giveback amount. If your giveback is $100 per month and your Social Security deposit was $1,800, your new deposit becomes $1,900. If you pay Medicare directly through a quarterly bill (common for beneficiaries who delayed Social Security), Medicare simply bills you less. There is no cash deposit to a separate account, no debit card, and nothing to spend. The money is already yours; the plan is reimbursing you for a bill you were already paying.

Mechanically, your Medicare Advantage plan pays CMS the giveback amount on your behalf each month. CMS then reduces what it withholds from Social Security (or what it bills you). The transfer happens behind the scenes, usually within 60 to 90 days of enrollment taking effect, and shows up on the Social Security COLA notice you receive each December.

Because the giveback is a reduction of a fixed premium, it has a hard ceiling. For 2026, the standard Part B premium is $202.90 per month. A plan can offer a $0 giveback, a $25 giveback, a $100 giveback, or the full $202.90 giveback, but it cannot pay you anything beyond the standard premium. If you pay an IRMAA surcharge for high income, the giveback is applied against your base premium only; the IRMAA portion is unaffected. If you owe a Part B late enrollment penalty, the giveback is applied before the penalty.

The 2027 Giveback Landscape: Growth, Then a Pullback

A June 2025 Johns Hopkins study published in JAMA Health Forum traced the giveback’s rise. The share of Medicare Advantage plans offering any Part B premium reduction rose from 4.3 percent in 2018 to nearly 19 percent in 2024. Enrollment responded. The researchers, analyzing more than 130 million enrollee-years, found that plans offering a giveback had 33.3 percent higher enrollment than otherwise-similar plans without one, and plans whose giveback covered more than 80 percent of the standard Part B premium saw enrollment gains of up to 245 percent. Beneficiaries, in other words, read the giveback as a strong signal and voted with their enrollment forms.

Three dynamics are now pulling the other way for 2027. First, final CMS Medicare Advantage rate benchmarks, while better than the proposed rule’s flat payment, delivered a smaller inflation-adjusted bump than carriers had planned for. Second, star rating methodology changes shifted bonus dollars that many plans had been using to fund giveback and supplemental benefits. Third, 2026 medical loss ratios ran hotter than carrier forecasts in several regions, particularly for GLP-1 utilization and post-acute care.

The result shows up in the September 2026 ANOC mailings. Multiple national carriers have reduced the giveback dollar amount on their most popular HMO plans, a handful have dropped the giveback entirely in favor of adding or restoring dental or OTC benefits, and a smaller number have raised the giveback as a market-share play in high-competition counties. The patterns are regional and plan-specific; there is no single "the giveback is going away" headline that applies everywhere.

The number to look at in your ANOC: The Medicare Part B premium buy-down or Part B premium reduction line in the "Changes to Your Plan’s Benefits" table. Compare the 2026 column to the 2027 column. A smaller 2027 number is a reduction. A blank 2027 column or "$0" is an elimination. Only a side-by-side comparison tells you the direction.

Who Qualifies (and Who Doesn’t)

Three conditions must be met to receive a Part B giveback.

1. You are enrolled in Medicare Parts A and B

The giveback is a reduction of a Part B premium you are paying. If you are enrolled only in Part A, there is nothing to reduce. Beneficiaries who delayed Part B because they had employer coverage can sign up for Part B during their Special Enrollment Period, enroll in a Medicare Advantage plan with a giveback, and begin receiving the benefit as soon as both are effective.

2. You pay the Part B premium yourself

This is where many beneficiaries are surprised. If your Part B premium is paid by Medicaid (for dual-eligibles) or by a Medicare Savings Program — Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB), Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB), or Qualifying Individual (QI) — the state is already covering your premium. There is nothing left to reduce, so the giveback has no effect for you. Dual-eligible Special Needs Plans (D-SNPs) do not offer meaningful Part B givebacks for this reason. If you are near the income threshold for one of these programs and have been paying the Part B premium out of pocket, the Medicare Savings Program is almost always worth more than a giveback.

3. You enroll in a Medicare Advantage plan that offers one in your service area

The Part B giveback exists only inside the Medicare Advantage program (Part C). Original Medicare does not offer a giveback. Medigap plans do not offer a giveback. Stand-alone Part D prescription drug plans do not offer a giveback. And not every Medicare Advantage plan offers one; the benefit is concentrated in competitive urban and suburban counties and in specific HMO or PPO products. A $130 giveback plan in Miami-Dade County, Florida does not mean your plan options in Butte County, Montana look anything like that.

How to Spot and Compare Giveback Plans During AEP 2026

The Medicare Plan Finder at medicare.gov is the authoritative, commission-free place to compare 2027 Medicare Advantage plans. 2027 plan data went live on October 1, 2026 and the AEP opened for enrollment on October 15. Here is the workflow a SHIP counselor would use.

Step 1 — Load your drug list and preferred providers

Before you can meaningfully compare plans on anything, including a giveback, Plan Finder needs your drug list and preferred pharmacies. Create a free account at medicare.gov (do not shop as a guest — the guest flow does not save your drug list between sessions). Enter each prescription, dose, and quantity exactly as filled. Add your preferred pharmacies, including whether you fill 90-day supplies by mail. Add your primary care physician and specialists; Plan Finder will flag plans whose network does not include them.

Step 2 — Pull every Medicare Advantage plan in your ZIP

Under "Find health & drug plans," select "Medicare Advantage Plan (Part C)" with your ZIP code and 2027 coverage year. Do not pre-filter by carrier; some of the strongest giveback plans in a market come from smaller regional carriers that beneficiaries do not search for by name.

Step 3 — Identify the giveback line

In each plan’s summary, look for a row labeled "Monthly Part B premium reduction," "Part B premium buy-down," or "Give-back amount." The number is the monthly dollar reduction. A row showing "$0" or "Not offered" means no giveback. A row showing "$65" means your Part B premium (deducted from Social Security or billed directly) drops by $65 a month. In the plan’s detailed benefits view, this line also appears in the "Premiums" section of the official Summary of Benefits.

Step 4 — Sort or filter

Plan Finder does not have a single "giveback" filter checkbox, but you can sort the plan list by monthly premium (ascending) and then scan the Part B premium reduction column. Plans that offer a negative effective premium — where the plan premium is $0 and the Part B giveback is positive — usually surface at the top.

Step 5 — Open the full Summary of Benefits for your top three

The giveback is one line. Thirty or forty other lines are also moving between 2026 and 2027: Part D deductible, specialty tier coinsurance, maximum out-of-pocket, dental allowance, OTC allowance, hearing aid allowance, in-network specialist copay, out-of-network copay, prior authorization lists. Open the full Summary of Benefits PDF for each finalist and compare them side by side. A $100-a-month giveback that comes with a $7,550 out-of-pocket maximum and no dental is a different plan than a $75-a-month giveback with a $5,500 out-of-pocket maximum and a $2,000 dental allowance; the math depends on what you expect to use.

Step 6 — If you want a human, call SHIP

Every state and territory funds a State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) with federal dollars. SHIP counselors are trained volunteers and staff who give one-on-one, commission-free help with Medicare decisions. They have access to the same Plan Finder data and can walk through the giveback and the tradeoffs with you. The national SHIP locator line is 1-877-839-2675, and SHIP counselors do not sell insurance — they do not earn a commission on any plan you pick.

The Three Tradeoffs You Shouldn’t Ignore

A giveback is not free money. The plan is funding the benefit from the same CMS payment pool it uses to fund dental, vision, OTC, drug subsidies, and provider networks. When a plan allocates more to a Part B giveback, something else is almost always smaller. The three places that most often show the tradeoff:

Tradeoff 1 — A higher maximum out-of-pocket

Medicare Advantage plans must cap your in-network out-of-pocket costs each year. The 2027 federal ceiling is $9,350 for in-network services and $14,000 combined for in- and out-of-network on PPOs. The ceiling is a maximum, not a required amount. Non-giveback plans in competitive markets routinely cap in-network costs at $4,000 to $6,500. Plans with large givebacks more often sit near the ceiling. If you have a chronic condition that drives high utilization, a $200 giveback that comes with a $9,350 cap can be dwarfed by $2,000 to $3,000 of additional cost sharing in a bad year.

Tradeoff 2 — A higher Part D drug deductible

Medicare Advantage prescription drug (MA-PD) plans can set a Part D deductible anywhere from $0 to the 2027 standard maximum of $615. Plans without a giveback often charge a $0 Part D deductible as a lead feature. Plans with large givebacks more often charge the full $615. On an $8,000 brand-name drug year that difference is small, because the new $2,100 Part D out-of-pocket cap will cover you either way. On a $600 generic drug year, that difference is the whole year’s drug spend.

Tradeoff 3 — Thinner supplemental benefits

Dental allowances, vision allowances, hearing aid allowances, and OTC flex cards vary from plan to plan. Non-giveback plans in competitive markets regularly offer $2,000 to $3,000 in annual dental coverage and OTC cards of $100 or more a quarter. Giveback-heavy plans tend to offer thinner supplemental packages — a $500 dental allowance, a smaller OTC card, or none at all. If a crown or a root canal is in your near future, the giveback math may already be lost.

A simple comparison worth doing: Multiply the giveback by 12. Add the plan premium (if any) times 12. Subtract the annual dental and OTC allowances you will actually use. Add your expected drug spend inside the plan. The result is a rough first pass at the true annual cost of each finalist, before accounting for the maximum-out-of-pocket downside.

The ANOC Line That Decides Your 2027 Giveback

Each September, every Medicare Advantage plan is required to mail enrollees an Annual Notice of Change describing what will change about the plan for the next year. For AEP 2026 (covering plan year 2027), that mailing landed in late September 2026. If you are currently in a Medicare Advantage plan with a giveback, the ANOC is where you find out whether yours survives.

The relevant line sits in the benefits-change comparison table and is labeled "Medicare Part B premium buy-down," "Monthly Part B premium reduction," or "Give-back amount," depending on the plan. The table shows a 2026 column and a 2027 column side by side.

  • Smaller number in 2027: your giveback is being reduced. Compare the new number to the plan’s improved benefits (if any). A $30/month reduction is $360 a year; it should be at least offset by other improvements to be a wash.
  • Blank or $0 in 2027: your giveback is being eliminated. The plan may still be a good plan, but your effective monthly cost just went up by the full prior giveback. Check your net Social Security deposit math.
  • Larger number in 2027: your giveback is being increased. This is rare in the 2027 cycle but does happen in high-competition markets. Verify that nothing on the opposite side of the ledger — MOOP, Part D deductible, specialist copays — got worse.
  • Line not present: your plan did not offer a giveback in 2026 and does not for 2027 either. Shop the AEP for a 2027 plan that does, if one is available in your county.

The ANOC also lists plan premium, maximum out-of-pocket, drug deductible, provider network tier changes, prior authorization list changes, and supplemental benefit changes. Looking at the giveback alone will mislead you. Looking at the full benefits-change table takes about ten minutes and is the single highest-leverage AEP step.

A Four-Step AEP Checklist

  1. Open your ANOC and find the Part B premium buy-down line. Note what is changing. If your current plan is non-renewing entirely, the ANOC says so in bold on the first page; a Special Enrollment Period is triggered automatically.
  2. Build your Plan Finder account at medicare.gov. Load every prescription, every pharmacy, every physician. Pull the 2027 Medicare Advantage plan list for your ZIP.
  3. Rank your top three or four plans by net cost, not by giveback. Net cost is the plan premium plus expected drug costs plus expected medical cost sharing, minus the giveback, minus the supplemental benefits you will actually use.
  4. Call SHIP (1-877-839-2675) if anything is unclear. One hour with a free, unbiased counselor is the cheapest Medicare advice you will ever receive. Confirm your decision, then enroll through medicare.gov or by calling 1-800-MEDICARE before December 7, 2026.

If you miss December 7, two safety valves remain. The Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period runs January 1 through March 31, 2027, and allows one switch between MA plans or from MA back to Original Medicare. A 5-star Special Enrollment Period lets you switch into a 5-star plan at almost any time of year, if one is available in your county.

The Bottom Line

The Part B giveback is a legitimate benefit worth looking at — $77 a month in average 2024 value is not nothing, and in competitive counties the number is routinely higher. The complication for AEP 2026 is that insurers are rebalancing their benefit packages for 2027, and in roughly seven out of ten plans the giveback and other supplemental benefits are getting smaller rather than larger. That does not make a 2027 giveback plan a bad choice. It makes the ANOC comparison and the full Summary of Benefits review the whole game, because the giveback that was worth $150 a month in 2026 may be $100 a month in 2027, and the plan next to it on the Plan Finder page may now offer a $125 giveback with better dental.

Three things do most of the work. Check your ANOC. Load your drugs and doctors into Plan Finder before you compare. Add up the full annual cost of each finalist — giveback plus plan premium plus expected drug cost plus expected cost sharing, minus the supplemental benefits you will actually use — not the headline giveback alone. Done in that order, the giveback becomes one input into a cost calculation rather than the whole decision.
