ACA Open Enrollment Just Opened: What the CMS Fact Sheet, KFF's 13% Premium Projection, and the Treasury Eligibility Rule Mean for Your 2027 Plan Shopping

By HealthCalc Team

Published November 2, 2026

12 min read

ACA Open Enrollment opened on Sunday, November 1, 2026 for the 2027 plan year. Three documents and one rule change settled over the final weekend before OEP, and together they define what shoppers see on HealthCare.gov this week.

First, the CMS Plan Year 2027 Marketplace Plans and Prices Fact Sheet released October 30, 2026 highlights the administration's affordability framing: more than 75% of HealthCare.gov consumers can find a plan with a premium of $50 or less after subsidies, the federal government now covers nearly 90% of the lowest-cost premium for eligible tax-credit recipients, and consumers will see an average of 35 HSA-eligible plans alongside roughly 100 plans in total from five to six insurers.

Second, KFF's analysis of the same CMS data projects the average 2027 marketplace premium will rise about 13% year-over-year, broadly consistent with the state Department of Insurance approvals that landed through October (median 18% at the benchmark Silver level, with eight states clearing 25% and three under 10%).

Third, the October 14, 2026 Treasury and IRS proposed regulations would restrict ACA premium tax credit eligibility to U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals, and certain lawfully present noncitizens starting in 2027, and add new verification requirements for advance payments. The rule is proposed, not final. KFF estimates that if finalized, it could leave about one million additional people uninsured over time.

Fourth — and this is the one you need to internalize before you log in — the enhanced premium tax credits remain unextended. The House-passed three-year extension (H.R. 1834, 230-196 in January 2026) has sat on the Senate calendar since February with no vote. The post-shutdown continuing resolution runs through December 11 and does not include an extension. HealthCare.gov is operating under pre-2021 subsidy rules, which means the 400% FPL cliff is back and the 8.5%-of-income cap is gone. Our October 26 piece on the final 2027 benchmark approvals walked through the household-level math; this piece is about what to do in Open Enrollment week one.

What the CMS October 30 Fact Sheet Actually Says

The administration's fact sheet packs its headline numbers into five affordability claims. Each has a specific interpretation that matters when you shop:

CMS claim What it actually means
75%+ of HealthCare.gov consumers can find a plan $50 or less True after premium tax credits are applied, for enrollees who qualify. Above 400% FPL (the restored cliff) you do not qualify, and the $50 figure does not apply. The CMS release does not break this down by income band.
Federal subsidy covers nearly 90% of lowest-cost premium True for the specific slice of eligible enrollees whose income puts them deep in the low-FPL range (roughly 100–150% FPL). It does not describe the typical subsidized enrollee at 250% or 350% FPL, whose expected contribution under the pre-2021 schedule is 8.33% and 9.83% of income respectively.
Average 35 HSA-eligible plans per shopper Up materially from prior years and real. HSA-eligible plans are generally Bronze-tier high-deductible plans; whether one works for your household depends on cash flow, not plan count. See the HSA-eligible Bronze plan guide.
~100 plans from 5–6 insurers on average True nationally; wide variance by rating area. Rural counties and tribal areas may still see 15–20 plans from two insurers. Check your actual plan count on your HealthCare.gov preview, which has been live since October 15.
Lowest-cost catastrophic plan averages ~10% below next-lowest metal-level plan Catastrophic plans are only available to enrollees under 30 or with a hardship exemption. For everyone else, the "cheapest" plan you can enroll in is the lowest-cost Bronze.
How to read these numbers correctly: every CMS affordability claim depends on tax-credit eligibility. The three groups whose affordability picture is not captured by the CMS framing: enrollees above 400% FPL (zero subsidy under pre-2021 rules), enrollees who could qualify for the credit but will fail the proposed-rule verification check if finalized, and enrollees whose chosen plan is above the benchmark Silver (because the subsidy is pegged to the benchmark, not to your plan).

Where KFF's 13% Projection Comes From and How It Reconciles with 18%

The headline figure in last week's benchmark-approval article was a median 18% increase in the state Department of Insurance–approved 2027 benchmark Silver. KFF's new figure is 13% average premium increase. Both can be true at the same time; they measure different things.

  • Median vs. average. The median tells you the middle state; the average is weighted by enrollment, and larger states (California, Texas, Florida, New York, Georgia) with smaller percentage increases pull the enrollment-weighted average down.
  • Benchmark Silver vs. all plans. Benchmark Silver drives the subsidy calculation and is a specific plan in each rating area. KFF's 13% averages across metal tiers (Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum) and plan types (HMO, PPO, EPO). Benchmark Silver tends to run slightly above the market average.
  • Pre- vs. post-rate-review. KFF's 13% uses CMS's October 30 figures, which are final after state rate review. The 18% from last week was approved-but-not-yet-published-nationally at the time.

Three drivers KFF cites in its analysis:

  1. Underlying medical and prescription drug cost trend. Insurers assumed a 10% underlying trend, up from the 8% that defined the recent decade. GLP-1 agonists (Wegovy, Zepbound, and now Ozempic for a wider range of indications) and continued specialty biologic expansion account for most of the acceleration.
  2. Hospital market concentration. KFF flags continued consolidation and its downstream effect on in-network prices. The No Surprises Act implementation is one factor driving negotiated rates upward at nominally in-network facilities.
  3. Enhanced-subsidy sunset adverse selection. Healthier subsidized enrollees are more price-sensitive and more likely to drop coverage; the remaining pool is sicker, which pushes rates further up. Insurer memoranda attribute 4–6% of the 2027 premium increase to this effect alone.

What the 13% number does not tell you is your household's net premium change. Net premium is benchmark minus subsidy, and the subsidy formula reverted to the pre-2021 schedule for 2027. If you are below 400% FPL and remain subsidy-eligible, your net premium will often be flat or modestly higher in absolute dollars (because the subsidy rises with the benchmark). If you are above 400% FPL, your subsidy drops to zero and you pay the full 13%-higher sticker.

The October 14 Treasury Proposed Rule: What It Changes, What It Does Not

Treasury and the IRS released proposed regulations on October 14, 2026 that would, starting with plan year 2027:

  1. Restrict PTC eligibility to U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals, and certain lawfully present noncitizens. The rule excludes categories of lawfully present noncitizens who were previously eligible, principally those under DACA, those with certain humanitarian parole statuses, and some other categories defined by the Department of Homeland Security.
  2. Add verification criteria for APTC payments. Advance payments of the premium tax credit would be subject to a tighter data-matching protocol with federal immigration databases and tax-return history. Mismatches would trigger a request for additional documentation and, if unresolved, loss of advance payment.
  3. Tighten verification generally aimed at reducing improper claims. The administration cites the September 2026 removal of 760,000 enrollees it alleged were improperly enrolled or did not exist.
Important: the rule is proposed, not final. Treasury has not published a comment-period close date in a way that locks the final effective date in 2027. Enrollees should enroll under the current rule and respond promptly to any marketplace data-matching request — the data-matching resolution guide walks through the process. KFF estimates that if the eligibility change is finalized as proposed, roughly one million additional people could become uninsured over time; HealthInsurance.org estimates a smaller first-year effect concentrated in a handful of states with higher DACA and parole-status populations.

What the proposed rule does not change:

  • Eligibility for Medicaid, CHIP, or Marketplace coverage without a subsidy (any lawfully present person can still enroll in a Marketplace plan).
  • The current eligibility rules for the 2027 plan year while the rule is pending.
  • Prior-year reconciliation (your 2026 PTC reconciliation on your 2026 tax return follows the current rules).

Your First-Week OEP Shopping Protocol

Open Enrollment week one is the right time to re-shop, not to auto-reenroll. Three traps make auto-reenrollment particularly risky this plan year:

  • Benchmark plan rotation. The benchmark Silver (second-lowest-cost Silver) rotates year to year. Your subsidy follows the new benchmark; your current plan does not. If your 2026 plan is no longer the second-lowest-cost Silver in 2027, your subsidy stays pegged to a plan that is not yours and your net premium can move dramatically in either direction.
  • Returning 400% FPL cliff. A projection error above 400% FPL now eliminates the entire subsidy. Auto-reenrollment uses stale income data from your last tax filing. If your 2024 income is on file and your 2027 projection is higher, the marketplace may extend an APTC you will owe back entirely at tax time.
  • Treasury verification uncertainty. Any data-matching flag now matters more than it did a year ago. The proposed rule tightens verification; even before it is finalized, CMS is operating with sharper flags.

The seven-step first-week protocol:

1Log into HealthCare.gov and pull your preview

If you have a HealthCare.gov account, your 2027 preview has been live since October 15. On the dashboard you will see your projected 2027 benchmark Silver, your estimated 2027 subsidy under the pre-2021 schedule, your current plan's draft 2027 premium, and — if your issuer discontinued or crosswalked your plan — the plan HealthCare.gov intends to default you to. State-based exchanges (California, New York, Washington, Massachusetts, Colorado, D.C., and 14 others) have equivalent previews on their own sites.

2Update your 2027 income projection

The default projected-income figure is usually your most recent tax-filed MAGI (likely tax year 2025). If your 2027 projected MAGI is materially different — new job, bonus swing, retirement, side income — update it now. The income-estimation walkthrough covers the specific lines (SE tax, HSA deduction, 401(k) deferral) that most often move the figure. If you are within 10% of the 400% FPL cliff, consider the year-end MAGI reduction playbook.

3Build your two shopping lists

Open Enrollment is time-pressured. Two documents turn plan comparison from an hour of searching to 15 minutes of checking: (a) a provider list — primary care, every specialist you saw in 2026, surgeons or facilities expected in 2027 — with NPI numbers; and (b) a prescription list with drug names, strengths, and quantities. The formulary matching protocol covers the drug-list workflow in detail.

4Re-sort plans by net cost under the new benchmark

HealthCare.gov sorts by premium by default. For the 2027 plan year, you want to sort by net cost (premium minus subsidy). Then re-sort by total expected annual cost (premium + deductible + estimated out-of-pocket for your usage pattern). The lowest-premium plan is often not the lowest-total-cost plan when deductibles swing from $500 to $8,000 across the metal tiers. The 2027 plan comparison guide covers the full re-sort workflow.

5Verify network and formulary against your two lists

Open each candidate plan's provider directory and formulary. Confirm that every doctor on your list is in-network for 2027 (not 2026; networks rotate at year-end), and that every drug on your list is on-formulary at a sustainable tier (Tier 1 or 2 for most people; Tier 3 or 4 only if you have budgeted for it). The ghost-network detection article covers the shortcuts for verifying directory accuracy.

6If you receive a data-matching request, respond within 90 days

Given the Treasury verification tightening, mismatches between your application data and federal databases are more likely to flag this year. The response window is 90 days from the request letter; miss it and your subsidy pauses. Document uploads go through the HealthCare.gov account portal. If you receive a request related to immigration status and are a lawfully present noncitizen, upload the relevant document (I-551, I-766, Section 240, etc.) and keep a copy of the submission confirmation.

7Mark December 15 and January 15 on your calendar

December 15, 2026 is the hard deadline for a January 1, 2027 effective date on HealthCare.gov. Auto-reenrollment runs on December 15 using your current application data. Miss December 15 and the earliest effective date is February 1, provided you enroll by January 15. If you have state-based coverage (New York, California, New Jersey, Washington D.C., Rhode Island), check your state's specific dates — several run through January 31 or beyond.

If an Enhanced-Subsidy Extension Passes Mid-OEP

The House-passed H.R. 1834 remains on the Senate calendar. If the Senate takes it up and passes some form of extension — a straight three-year renewal, a two-year compromise, or a hybrid with means-testing — CMS has indicated HealthCare.gov will update subsidy figures retroactively to November 1, 2026 and push notifications to affected enrollees.

Practically, for someone who followed the seven-step protocol:

  • Your selected plan is unaffected. The subsidy rises; your net cost falls.
  • If a different plan becomes more attractive after the subsidy change (Gold often becomes competitive with Silver when net cost is capped at 8.5%), you can re-select without penalty through January 15, 2027.
  • If you are above 400% FPL under the pre-2021 schedule and an extension restores the 8.5% cap, your subsidy goes from zero to meaningful. Re-shopping is strongly advisable in this case.

The congressional calendar supports an extension vote in November or December if the Senate schedules floor time; the mid-term election cycle argues for either side pushing it to year-end. Watch congressional scheduling, not CMS announcements — CMS has no discretion over the subsidy formula and will mechanically implement whatever Congress enacts.

Three Mistakes to Avoid in Week One

Mistake 1: Reading the $50 CMS claim as the typical experience

The 75%-can-get-$50-or-less figure applies to enrollees who qualify for the premium tax credit under the restored pre-2021 schedule. Above 400% FPL, you pay full sticker on the 2027 approved rate, which averages 13% higher nationally and more than 25% higher in eight states. The CMS fact sheet does not break down the $50 figure by income band, so the typical experience of a 450% FPL enrollee — a four-figure monthly premium — is invisible in the summary.

Mistake 2: Treating the Treasury rule as if it is already in force

The proposed regulations do not take effect automatically. The current eligibility rules apply through at least the open comment period and finalization. If you are lawfully present but in a category the proposed rule would exclude, enroll under current rules and let the data-matching protocol run its course. If the rule is finalized before your plan year starts, CMS will issue transition guidance; you will not retroactively lose coverage you have already paid for.

Mistake 3: Letting benchmark rotation reshape your subsidy silently

If your 2026 plan was the second-lowest-cost Silver (benchmark) in your rating area and a different plan is benchmark in 2027, your subsidy stays pegged to the new benchmark but your plan's premium follows its own 2027 approved rate. The two can move in opposite directions. On the November 1 shopping session, re-sort every candidate plan by net cost, not by gross premium, and compare the net cost of your 2026 plan under 2027 subsidy to the net cost of the new benchmark Silver.
