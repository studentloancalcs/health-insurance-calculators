How to Appeal Medicare Hospital Observation Status in 2026: Protect Your Skilled Nursing Facility Coverage After Alexander v. Azar

By HealthCalc Team

Published October 16, 2026

11 min read

The hospital bed looks identical, the IV line is the same, the nurses walk the same halls, and the attending physician makes the same morning rounds. On paper, though, the Medicare patient in bed A is an inpatient and the patient in bed B is "under observation" — a billing distinction that becomes catastrophic the moment either of them needs a stint at a skilled nursing facility to recover.

Observation status is Medicare's quietest expensive mistake. The three-day inpatient rule that unlocks Part A coverage for a SNF stay does not count observation days, so a patient who spent five midnights in a hospital bed under observation and discharges to a rehab facility can face a bill of several thousand dollars a week with no Part A help at all. Add the Part B cost-sharing on each individual observation service — which escapes the single Part A inpatient deductible — and the economics shift sharply against the patient.

For years there was no formal way to challenge an observation classification. The Alexander v. Azar litigation (which continued as Barrows v. Becerra) changed that. Original Medicare beneficiaries who were admitted as inpatient and then reclassified to observation now have the right to an expedited administrative appeal, and CMS implemented the appeal process beginning in 2024. Combined with the two-midnight rule and the underlying Part A and B cost math, the appeal is now a real lever for recovering SNF coverage that would otherwise be lost.

Here is how the mechanism actually works in 2026 — who qualifies, which paperwork proves the case, and the hospital-level moves to make before discharge.

Inpatient Versus Observation: The Billing Difference That Changes Everything Downstream

Medicare pays hospitals under two different claim types for a stay that looks the same from the patient's bed. An inpatient admission is billed under Part A, which is a single bundled payment covering the room, nursing, meals, routine labs, and most ancillary services. The patient pays a single Part A deductible — $1,676 for benefit periods beginning in 2026 — and the rest of the inpatient hospital cost is covered until day 60. Observation stays are billed under Part B as outpatient services. Each lab, each imaging study, each physician visit is a separate line item, and the patient pays 20 percent coinsurance on each after the Part B deductible is met.

For a short stay that itemizes to a modest total, Part B can actually be cheaper than Part A because the deductible is smaller. For a long or complex observation stay with multiple imaging studies, a cardiac workup, and specialist consults, the stacked 20 percent coinsurance exceeds the single Part A deductible. In 2026 CMS data shows the breakeven point lands somewhere around a three-to-four-day stay with moderate testing — longer than that, Part A is almost always financially better for the patient.

But the real stakes are not inside the hospital bill. They are at the next facility.

The SNF chain reaction. Medicare Part A covers a skilled nursing facility stay only after three consecutive calendar midnights of inpatient hospital care, not counting the discharge day. Observation midnights do not count. A patient who needs post-hospital rehabilitation after a hip replacement, stroke, serious infection, or heart event can face a $400 to $700 daily SNF bill with no Part A help at all if the preceding hospital stay was observation. Over three weeks of rehab, that is a $12,000 to $20,000 out-of-pocket bill that would have been fully covered on days one through twenty of a Part A SNF benefit after a qualifying inpatient stay.

The MOON Notice: What Must Be Delivered and When

The Medicare Outpatient Observation Notice is a standardized disclosure introduced under the NOTICE Act and refined through later CMS rulemaking. If a Medicare beneficiary has been receiving observation services for more than 24 hours, the hospital must deliver the MOON — both in writing and orally — within 36 hours of the start of observation or at discharge, whichever comes first.

The notice does five things:

  • Tells the beneficiary that the services received are being classified as outpatient observation rather than inpatient admission.
  • Explains that observation time does not count toward the three-midnight requirement for skilled nursing facility coverage under Part A.
  • Lists expected Part B cost sharing categories, including coinsurance on each service and a separate charge for any self-administered drugs the hospital bills (routine take-home prescriptions are generally not covered under Part B at all).
  • Describes the general reason for observation — typically a short clinical note stating that the attending physician does not expect a stay of at least two midnights.
  • Requires the beneficiary (or an authorized representative) to sign to acknowledge receipt, and the hospital staff member delivering the oral explanation to co-sign.

The MOON is a disclosure, not a consent form. Signing it does not waive any appeal rights, and refusing to sign does not change the classification. The signed MOON is, however, the single most important document for a later appeal because it establishes the exact date and time the hospital identified observation status, which anchors every deadline that follows.

Keep the signed MOON. Many hospitals deliver the MOON in the room during a shift change or at discharge, and the beneficiary or family member stuffs it in a bag. Scan or photograph it before leaving the hospital. The signed-and-dated original is the most powerful piece of evidence in the Alexander appeal, in the Medicare Summary Notice appeal, and in any dispute with the SNF about who owes what.

The Two-Midnight Rule and Why It Favors Inpatient Status More Often Than Hospitals Apply It

CMS's two-midnight rule, in effect since 2013 and refined through subsequent rulemaking, sets the physician's expected length of stay as the central factor in the inpatient-versus-observation decision. If, at the time of admission, the admitting physician expected the patient to require hospital care spanning at least two midnights, the stay is presumptively appropriate for Part A inpatient billing. If the expected stay is shorter, observation is the default. The rule shifted the decision from a backward-looking severity-of-illness test to a forward-looking expectation of length-of-stay.

Three features of the current rule matter for an appeal.

1. The documented expectation at admission controls

The two-midnight benchmark is anchored to what the admitting physician wrote in the history and physical at the time of admission. If that note documents a reasonable expectation of two or more midnights — based on the patient's condition, planned testing or treatment, or anticipated clinical course — the stay is presumptively inpatient even if the patient recovers faster than expected and goes home after a single midnight.

2. Case-by-case exceptions below two midnights

CMS allows inpatient admission for stays shorter than two midnights when the physician documents a specific clinical reason: new mechanical ventilation, a rare procedure, or similar circumstances. This narrow carve-out is often misunderstood as the only path to inpatient status for short stays.

3. The MA extension under CMS-4201-F

Beginning January 1, 2024, CMS-4201-F extended the two-midnight rule to Medicare Advantage plans. MA plans are no longer allowed to apply their own internal medical-necessity screens to override the admitting physician's documented two-midnight expectation. In practice, this means a Medicare Advantage enrollee whose stay was downgraded from inpatient to observation can point to the specific CMS regulation requiring the MA plan to defer to the admitting physician's reasonable expectation.

The practical takeaway: if the admitting physician's history and physical shows a reasonable expectation of two or more midnights, and the hospital's utilization-management team overrode that expectation and reclassified the stay to observation, the beneficiary almost always has grounds to appeal.

Alexander v. Azar: What the Case Decided and Who It Covers

Alexander v. Azar, which continued through later proceedings as Barrows v. Becerra, was a nationwide class action tried in the District of Connecticut and affirmed by the Second Circuit Court of Appeals. The plaintiffs were Original Medicare beneficiaries whose hospital stays had been initially admitted as inpatient and then reclassified to observation by hospital utilization-review teams. The court held that these beneficiaries had a protected property interest in the inpatient classification and were entitled, under the Due Process Clause, to an administrative appeal of the reclassification.

CMS implemented the resulting appeal process in rulemaking that took effect beginning in 2024. The appeal operates in two tracks.

Track Who qualifies Timing
Expedited (contemporaneous) Current inpatient-to-observation reclassification while still in the hospital or shortly after discharge Request promptly; a Beneficiary and Family Centered Care Quality Improvement Organization (BFCC-QIO) reviews on an expedited basis
Retrospective (class-wide) Original Medicare beneficiaries whose stays were reclassified from inpatient to observation on or after January 1, 2009, were three or more consecutive days, and resulted in excess Part B cost sharing or a denied SNF claim Filed through the regular Medicare administrative appeal channels using Alexander-specific guidance

The appeal does not cover a stay that was coded as observation from the start. The Alexander class is defined by the reclassification — an initial inpatient order that was later changed by hospital utilization review. Beneficiaries whose stays were observation from the first hour must rely on other avenues: a request to the attending physician for a rebilling under condition code 44 (which must happen before discharge), a Medicare Summary Notice appeal if a specific service was denied, or a traditional Part A claim denial appeal if the hospital attempted a Part A claim and Medicare paid it as Part B.

What a favorable decision does

A favorable appeal decision reclassifies the stay to inpatient retroactively. That triggers several things at once:

  • The hospital rebills the stay under Part A. The patient's Part B cost sharing on observation services is refunded; the Part A deductible is applied in its place.
  • The stay now counts toward the three-midnight requirement. If the patient went on to a SNF, the SNF claim can be resubmitted under Part A for retroactive payment.
  • If the SNF was never claimed because the patient paid privately, Medicare pays the SNF the Part A rate and the SNF refunds the patient's private payment, subject to coordination through the SNF's billing office.

The 2026 Appeal Workflow, Start to Finish

The appeal has slightly different mechanics depending on whether the beneficiary is still in the hospital, recently discharged, or looking at a long-closed stay. The common steps are below.

Step 1 — Gather the six core documents

Before filing anything, assemble:

  1. The signed MOON notice with its date and time of delivery.
  2. The hospital discharge summary showing admission date, discharge date, and the classification applied.
  3. The itemized hospital bill (UB-04 line detail). This shows the HCPCS and revenue codes billed and, critically, the exact date a reclassification occurred if there was one.
  4. The admitting physician's history and physical, which anchors the two-midnight expectation.
  5. The hospital utilization-review notes. These must be produced on written request to the hospital's medical records department under HIPAA. They show which criterion (often Milliman or InterQual) the hospital applied to reclassify.
  6. Any SNF bill, SNF denial letter, or Medicare Summary Notice showing a denied SNF claim or a denied inpatient claim.

Step 2 — Request a contemporaneous reclassification if still in the hospital

If the beneficiary is still admitted, the fastest lever is in the hospital, not in an administrative tribunal. Ask the attending physician to document the two-midnight expectation if clinically supported, and ask the case manager to re-review the utilization-management decision. Hospitals that use condition code 44 can convert an inpatient admission to observation while the patient is still admitted; the reverse (observation to inpatient) requires a new physician order. The attending physician is the person who can write that order.

Step 3 — For Medicare Advantage, request an expedited organization determination

MA enrollees whose stay has been downgraded to observation should request an expedited organization determination under 42 CFR 422.566. The MA plan has 72 hours to respond, or 24 hours if the beneficiary's health requires it. The written request should cite CMS-4201-F and ask the plan to apply the two-midnight rule as federally required. If the plan denies, the next level is an independent review entity, then an administrative law judge.

Step 4 — File the Alexander appeal for Original Medicare reclassifications

For Original Medicare beneficiaries whose stays were initially inpatient and then reclassified to observation, the Alexander appeal is filed through the standard Medicare appeals pathway with specific class-defining language. The initial redetermination is handled by the Medicare Administrative Contractor; the next level is a qualified independent contractor. For stays with potential SNF loss above the amount-in-controversy threshold, the beneficiary can escalate to an administrative law judge hearing, and above that to the Medicare Appeals Council.

Step 5 — Appeal the SNF claim denial separately if applicable

If a SNF claim was denied because of lack of a qualifying hospital stay, the SNF denial itself is appealable. A successful hospital-stay reclassification generally resolves the SNF denial, but the two appeals run on parallel tracks and both should be filed if the SNF bills exceed the Part A deductible.

Special Situations and Common Mistakes

Observation from the start, never inpatient

If the stay was coded as observation from the first order and never carried an inpatient admission, the Alexander class does not apply. The remaining levers are (1) condition code 44 conversion before discharge, (2) a Medicare Summary Notice appeal on each service that was denied as not medically necessary, and (3) in a narrow set of cases, a hospital-level billing complaint if the attending physician's documentation supported inpatient status and the hospital overrode it.

Medicare Advantage with a SNF three-day waiver

Many Medicare Advantage plans waive the three-day rule entirely for SNF coverage, meaning the observation-versus-inpatient debate does not control SNF eligibility. Read the Evidence of Coverage carefully; a plan that waives the three-day requirement can still deny the SNF stay on medical-necessity grounds, which is a separate (and separately appealable) question.

Accountable Care Organization three-day waiver

Original Medicare beneficiaries attributed to a Medicare Shared Savings Program accountable care organization may qualify for a nationwide three-day waiver when discharged to a qualifying SNF. The waiver is applied by the hospital or ACO; the beneficiary does not file for it, but confirming attribution with the physician's practice can shortcut the entire observation debate.

Skilled nursing facility billed the patient directly

If the SNF has already billed the patient at the private-pay rate because of the missing inpatient qualifying stay, the SNF must refund any amount it later receives from Medicare when the appeal succeeds. Do not sign away appeal rights on SNF intake paperwork; keep SNF invoices and payment receipts for the duration of the appeal.

Running the Part B cost math on the observation bill

Even without the SNF question, an observation stay with many stacked Part B services can exceed the Part A deductible by itself. A quick spreadsheet of each line item at 20 percent coinsurance against the single Part A deductible is often enough to establish that the economic consequence of the reclassification exceeded the amount-in-controversy threshold for an administrative law judge appeal.

The Bottom Line

Observation status was, for years, Medicare's quietest cost-shifter. A hospital bed looks like a hospital bed, so beneficiaries and families rarely noticed the distinction until the SNF bill arrived. Alexander v. Azar gave Original Medicare beneficiaries a real appeal when hospital utilization review reclassified an initial inpatient admission to observation, and CMS-4201-F extended the two-midnight rule to Medicare Advantage, cutting off the private medical-necessity screens that MA plans used to run around it.

The practical response is a short checklist. Keep the signed MOON notice. Ask the admitting physician to document the two-midnight expectation. Request the utilization-review notes in writing before leaving the hospital. If the stay is reclassified from inpatient to observation, file the Alexander appeal in Original Medicare or the expedited organization determination in Medicare Advantage. Keep the SNF bill and run the Part B observation math as a parallel appeal. The documents and deadlines are not forgiving, but the levers are now in place for the first time, and the dollars at stake — often five figures for a post-hospital rehab stay — justify the paperwork.
