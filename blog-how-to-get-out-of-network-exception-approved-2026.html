How to Get an Out-of-Network Exception Approved in 2026: The Network Gap Exception Playbook, Medical Necessity Documentation, and NSA Continuity of Care

By HealthCalc Team

Published September 12, 2026

13 min read

An out-of-network specialist visit that would cost $325 in network can hit $2,800 out of network. A surgery that runs $3,400 in network can run $28,000. The gap is not the surgery — it is the two words "in-network," and in 2026 there is a formal mechanism to close it: the network gap exception.

A gap exception (also called a network exception, gap waiver, or in-network exception) is a decision by your health plan to apply in-network cost sharing to a specific out-of-network provider or service because the plan cannot supply an adequate in-network alternative. When approved, your deductible, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket maximum all apply as if the provider were in the network. This guide walks through the 2026 request in the order that actually works: whether you even need one (the No Surprises Act has already given you in-network rates in three situations), the four medical-necessity arguments that get approvals, the mental health parity leverage that turns behavioral health denials into approvals, the exact documentation to attach, and the two-tier appeal path when the plan says no.

Step 1: Check Whether You Already Have In-Network Cost Sharing

Before you request an exception, confirm you actually need one. The No Surprises Act (NSA), fully in force in 2026, already gives you in-network cost sharing automatically in three situations. If any of these apply, do not file a gap exception — file the claim as usual and dispute any bill that exceeds in-network cost sharing.

Situation What the NSA Guarantees Your Cost Sharing
Emergency services (any facility, including air/ground ambulance and post-stabilization care) In-network cost sharing; balance billing prohibited In-network deductible, coinsurance, and OOP max
Out-of-network providers at an in-network facility (anesthesia, radiology, pathology, assistant surgeon, hospitalist) In-network cost sharing unless you signed the NSA notice-and-consent form In-network deductible, coinsurance, and OOP max
Continuity of care after in-network provider is terminated for non-cause reasons Up to 90 days of continued care at in-network rates for a qualifying condition In-network deductible, coinsurance, and OOP max during the transition

The continuity-of-care right is the one most people don't know about. If your primary care doctor, oncologist, obstetrician, or behavioral health provider is dropped from the network for anything other than misconduct — a contract expired, the group left the plan, an insurer switched networks — the plan must notify you within 15 days and let you request up to 90 additional days at in-network cost sharing for a serious and complex condition, an ongoing course of institutional or inpatient care, a scheduled non-elective surgery, pregnancy, or terminal illness. Send the request in writing within the plan's stated response window and reference the NSA continuity-of-care provisions specifically.

Cost-sharing math still works normally: "In-network cost sharing" means your in-network deductible, in-network coinsurance percentage, and in-network out-of-pocket maximum. It doesn't mean $0. Use the plan cost calculator below to model what you'll actually pay before or after the exception.

Step 2: Confirm You Need an Exception (Not a Different Fix)

Gap exceptions are the right tool for planned, non-emergency care with a specific out-of-network provider you want to use. If any of the following describe your situation, a different fix is faster or better:

  • You just want a referral to a specialist: ask for an in-network referral first — most PPO and EPO plans have unlisted specialists in the wider network directory that don't appear in the plan's spotlight lists.
  • The provider says they take your insurance but the plan's directory doesn't list them: ask both the provider and the plan to verify the tax ID and the exact plan/product name (a group can be in-network for the parent insurer's PPO and out-of-network for a marketplace HMO under the same brand).
  • You need a one-off specialty medication: a formulary exception (drug coverage), not a network exception, is the right lever.
  • The provider is in-network for the medical plan but not the behavioral health carve-out: confirm which entity manages behavioral health benefits (often a separate MBHO) and file with that entity.

Step 3: Build the Medical Necessity Case

Every plan's gap exception form asks the same underlying question: is there a comparable in-network provider who can deliver equivalent care within a reasonable time? Your job is to answer no with evidence. Four arguments get approvals in 2026:

3a. No In-Network Provider Has the Required Specialty or Subspecialty

Board-certified pediatric endocrinologists, gender-affirming surgeons, movement disorder neurologists, complex-spine specialists, adolescent eating disorder programs — many subspecialties have thin coverage in any given network. Call every in-network name in the plan's directory and log the outcome: "Dr. Smith is a general endocrinologist and doesn't treat pediatric pituitary disorders" is a stronger answer than "the directory has 40 endocrinologists."

3b. No In-Network Provider Is Accepting New Patients or Available Within a Clinically Reasonable Time

Call the top ten in-network providers on the plan's list. Log the date, the name of the person you spoke with, whether they are accepting new patients, and their next available appointment. If everyone available is more than 60 days out for a condition that requires care within 30, you have a network adequacy problem, and the plan is obligated to address it.

3c. The Out-of-Network Provider Has Established Care and Continuity Is Clinically Important

Applies when you have a preexisting therapeutic relationship — an ongoing oncology treatment plan, a stable psychiatric medication regimen, a transplant follow-up. Attach records showing the duration and complexity of the relationship. Under the NSA's continuity-of-care provisions, this argument overlaps with an automatic in-network rate for up to 90 days after a network termination, but the exception request extends the logic to broader situations where the NSA doesn't strictly apply.

3d. The In-Network Providers Do Not Have the Required Facility, Equipment, or Program Accreditation

Center-of-Excellence bariatric programs, CAR-T cell therapy sites, proton therapy centers, comprehensive stroke centers, and MBSAQIP-accredited surgical programs all have narrow lists of qualifying facilities. If the treatment your physician recommends requires an accredited program and none is in-network within reasonable geography, cite the accreditation standard by name.

Network adequacy is a legal standard, not a courtesy: Marketplace plans must meet CMS network adequacy standards, which include time and distance standards by specialty and appointment wait time standards. If the plan cannot meet those standards for your care, you have a stronger argument for an exception and, if denied, a stronger external review case. Document your calls and the plan's directory searches — screenshots and call logs are your evidence.

Step 4: The Mental Health Parity Angle

The Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA), backed by the 2024 final rule enforced throughout 2026, holds plans to a strict standard: any network adequacy criterion, prior authorization rule, or cost-sharing structure applied to mental health and substance use disorder benefits must be no more restrictive than the standard applied to medical/surgical benefits. If a plan's medical/surgical network has one cardiologist per 5,000 members but only one psychiatrist per 15,000 members, that disparity is itself a parity problem.

Behavioral health gap exception denials are among the most reversed on external review because of this. When drafting a behavioral health request:

  • Cite MHPAEA and the 2024 final rule by name.
  • Document your in-network calls with dates, wait times, and refusal reasons ("not accepting new patients," "does not treat eating disorders," "does not offer telehealth").
  • Ask the plan in writing for its "Non-Quantitative Treatment Limitation (NQTL) comparative analysis" for network composition — this is a document plans are required to produce on request and rarely can produce cleanly.
  • If a state insurance department is your regulator, mention that you will file a parity complaint if the exception is denied without adequate justification.

Step 5: Assemble the Request Package

A gap exception request that gets approved on the first read includes six things, packaged as a single PDF with a one-page cover letter that summarizes the ask in the first paragraph:

  1. The cover letter — member name, ID, group number, date of birth, requested service dates, out-of-network provider name and NPI, and one clear sentence about why the exception is needed.
  2. Physician letter of medical necessity — your treating physician's statement that the specific out-of-network provider is medically necessary, referencing the clinical criteria and why in-network options are inadequate.
  3. Network inadequacy log — a table with the in-network provider names you called, the phone numbers, dates, the person you spoke with, and the disposition ("full panel," "wait 12 weeks," "doesn't treat pediatric," etc.).
  4. CPT and ICD-10 codes — the specific procedure codes and diagnosis codes for the requested service, obtained from the treating provider's office in advance.
  5. Provider details — NPI, tax ID, and a written quote or estimate of charges from the out-of-network provider.
  6. Clinical records — a concise packet showing the relevant history (not your entire chart), any prior authorization approvals in place, and imaging/pathology summaries where relevant.

Send by both the plan's stated method (portal upload or fax to the utilization management department) and by certified mail to the member services address on your card. Get a case number and a decision timeline before you hang up.

Step 6: Decision Timelines and When to Push

Under the CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F), phased implementation through 2026 and full compliance in 2027 for Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP, and marketplace plans, standard prior authorization requests must be decided within 7 calendar days and urgent requests within 72 hours. Many self-funded ERISA plans have adopted comparable timelines voluntarily.

For non-urgent gap exceptions, submit at least 14 days before the scheduled service. For urgent situations — deteriorating symptoms, scheduled surgery within days, or an active mental health crisis — mark the request URGENT on every page, attach physician documentation of the timeline, and cite the 72-hour standard. If the plan misses its own deadline, that itself is grounds in most states for automatic authorization or expedited external review.

Follow up on day 3 and day 5. Portals lose things. Faxes fail. Call, quote your case number, and get the reviewer's name and the current status. A logged call trail is what protects you when you have to appeal.

Step 7: The Appeal Path When Denied

Denials are common on the first pass and reversed at meaningful rates on appeal, especially with additional documentation. Two paths run in parallel:

Internal Appeal (first)

Every plan must offer at least one level of internal appeal, and most offer two. The deadline is usually 180 days from denial. Attach: additional physician letters, updated network-inadequacy documentation, any new clinical developments, and a specific citation of the plan document sections and federal law you believe were misapplied. Ask in writing for the reviewer's clinical credentials — if the denial was based on medical necessity, the appeal must be reviewed by a physician in the same or a similar specialty.

External Review (parallel or after)

Once the internal appeal is denied — or the plan misses its deadline — you have the right to an external review by an Independent Review Organization (IRO). Fully insured plans go through your state Department of Insurance; self-funded ERISA plans use a federally administered process. Independent reviewers overturn gap exception denials on network adequacy grounds at roughly 40% to 60% when the request is well documented, and behavioral health parity denials are overturned at higher rates. External reviews are free to the member and usually decided within 45 days (72 hours for expedited urgent cases). The IRO's decision is binding on the plan.

Step 8: If All Else Fails — Negotiate Cash

If the exception is denied and appeals are exhausted, the fallback is to negotiate the provider's charge directly. Out-of-network providers often accept 40% to 60% of billed charges from self-pay patients paying at time of service, and hospitals frequently have written financial assistance policies (required for nonprofit hospitals under IRS 501(r) rules) that can eliminate the balance for households under specific income thresholds. Get any written self-pay agreement before service begins, and pay from your HSA if you have one — the tax treatment cuts roughly 30% off whatever number you negotiate.

The Full Framework in Order

  1. Confirm the NSA doesn't already give you in-network cost sharing (emergency, out-of-network provider at in-network facility, or continuity of care after network termination).
  2. Confirm a gap exception is the right tool — not a directory error, formulary exception, or behavioral health carve-out issue.
  3. Build the medical necessity case from one or more of the four winning arguments (subspecialty, availability, continuity, or accreditation).
  4. Add the mental health parity layer if the request is for behavioral health or substance use care.
  5. Assemble the six-part request package and submit by both portal and certified mail.
  6. Follow up within decision timelines (7 days standard, 72 hours urgent).
  7. Appeal internally with additional documentation if denied, then to external review through your state DOI or the federal process.
  8. Negotiate cash as the last resort if all approvals fail.

Network gap exceptions are one of the few areas where a well-documented request materially changes the outcome. In 2026, between the CMS prior authorization rule, the 2024 MHPAEA final rule, and the NSA continuity-of-care right, the paper trail has more leverage than most members realize. Build it once, use it in every follow-up, and treat the appeal as an expected step rather than a defeat.
