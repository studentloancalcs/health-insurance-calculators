How to Use the Medigap Birthday Rule to Switch Medicare Supplement Plans Without Underwriting (2026 Guide)

By HealthCalc Team

Published October 24, 2026

11 min read

Medicare Supplement (Medigap) premiums for the same plan letter can vary by 30% to 60% between carriers in the same ZIP code. Plan G is Plan G is Plan G — the benefits are standardized by federal law — but the price is not. In most states, moving from an expensive Plan G to a cheaper carrier's Plan G is a trip through medical underwriting: health questions, prescription-drug checks, sometimes an attending physician statement. If your health has deteriorated since you first enrolled, the carrier can decline you outright, surcharge you, or impose a pre-existing-condition waiting period. That is why most people never switch, and why premium creep eats roughly 5% to 8% a year on a typical attained-age-rated Medigap plan.

In 13 states, a different rule applies. Around your birthday each year, your current Medigap policy triggers a guaranteed-issue window in which you can switch to a competing plan of the same or lesser benefits without any health questions. The carrier must accept you, cover your pre-existing conditions, and quote you the standard rate for your age, gender, and tobacco status. This is the Medigap birthday rule, and if you live in one of the states that offers it, it is one of the most under-used levers in Medicare consumer protection.

This guide covers the 13 states that have the birthday rule as of October 2026, the window length and same-or-lesser-benefit nuance in each, the three-step workflow for switching, and the common mistakes that get people stuck. What the Birthday Rule Actually Does

The Medigap birthday rule is state law, not federal law. The federal Medicare statute provides a one-time, six-month Medigap Open Enrollment Period that begins the first month you are age 65 or older and enrolled in Part B. During that initial window, every Medigap plan is guaranteed-issue. After it ends, federal law does not require carriers to accept you at all. States are free to layer additional protections on top.

A birthday rule state does exactly that. It carves out an annual window, triggered by the policyholder's birthday, during which carriers licensed to sell Medigap in the state must accept switch applications on a guaranteed-issue basis, subject to one or two limitations:

  • Same-or-lesser-benefit limitation. In most states, you can move to a Medigap plan with the same or fewer benefits than your current plan, but you cannot upgrade. A Plan G enrollee can move to another carrier's Plan G or step down to Plan N. A Plan N enrollee cannot move up to Plan G without underwriting.
  • Window-length limitation. The window is finite — typically 30 to 93 days — and expires without renewal. A missed window waits a year.
  • Continuous coverage requirement. You must have an existing Medigap policy at the time you apply. A Medicare Advantage enrollee cannot use the birthday rule to switch to Medigap — different pathways cover that scenario.

Within those limits, the rule is a yearly chance to shop Medigap pricing without health gatekeeping, which for anyone whose health has changed since initial enrollment is a meaningful advantage.

States With a Medigap Birthday Rule in 2026

As of October 2026, 13 states have a Medigap birthday rule or an analogous annual guaranteed-issue window. Three were added in 2026 alone: New Mexico (signed into law early 2026), West Virginia (effective mid-2026), and Delaware (effective January 2026). Virginia expanded its existing continuous-switch protections in July 2026. Below is the state-by-state detail that matters when you plan your window.

Maryland 30 days Starts on birthday Same benefits, same carrier (continuous open enrollment separate)
Virginia 60 days Starts on birthday (effective July 2026) Same or lesser benefits
West Virginia 60 days Starts on birthday (effective 2026) Same or lesser benefits

Delaware 60 days Starts on birthday (effective January 2026) Same or lesser benefits

Washington state operates on a different model entirely: year-round guaranteed-issue for most Medigap plans at the standardized attained-age rate, regardless of birthday. If you live in Washington, you can switch any time of year, which makes the birthday rule discussion mostly academic.

Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine, and New York have continuous-open-enrollment protections of various strengths. In practice, Medigap guaranteed-issue in those states is broader than a birthday window, so if you live there, you have more flexibility than this article describes — but confirm the details with your state Department of Insurance before relying on it.

Confirm your window: State laws change. The 2026 additions came from state legislatures acting throughout the year, and other states are considering similar bills. Before you plan a switch, call your state Department of Insurance or your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) at 1-877-839-2675 and confirm the current window length, timing, and switch rule.

Why This Rule Saves Money

Medigap pricing is one of the strangest consumer markets in American insurance. The plans themselves are standardized — Plan A, Plan B, Plan G, Plan N, and so on — so every carrier's Plan G covers exactly the same services at exactly the same cost-sharing. There is no benefit shopping to do within a plan letter. All of the shopping is about price.

And the price variance is enormous. In a typical California ZIP code, a 70-year-old nonsmoking woman might see Plan G quotes ranging from $140 a month at the cheapest carrier to $250 a month at the most expensive — a 78% spread for the identical policy. In Texas, which is not a birthday rule state, that same consumer might see a similar spread but has no annual mechanism to shop it. Over a 20-year Medicare retirement, choosing the wrong carrier can cost $25,000 or more in cumulative premium with no additional benefit.

The birthday rule turns that spread into a competitive market. Each year, you can look at the current rates in your state, pick the cheapest community-rated or well-stabilized attained-age-rated plan at the same benefit level, and switch without health underwriting. Your actual savings depend on your current carrier, your age band, and the specific plan letter, but a $50-to-$100-a-month premium reduction is not unusual for someone who has been with the same carrier for five or more years and never shopped.

Same-or-Lesser-Benefit: What That Actually Means

The same-or-lesser-benefit limitation is the single most common source of confusion with the birthday rule. The intent is to prevent people from gaming the system — starting out on a cheap Plan K (which has a 50% cost share on most services) and then upgrading to a comprehensive Plan G once they develop a serious condition — by requiring that any switch be either lateral or downward.

Medigap plans have a rough benefit hierarchy. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners has a standardized ranking for same-or-lesser-benefit determinations:

  1. Plan F (most comprehensive — closed to new enrollees since 2020 but still held by many)
  2. Plan C (comprehensive — also closed to new enrollees since 2020)
  3. Plan G
  4. Plan N
  5. Plan D
  6. Plan M
  7. Plan A
  8. Plan B
  9. High-Deductible Plan G
  10. High-Deductible Plan F (closed to new enrollees)
  11. Plan L
  12. Plan K

You can switch to any plan at the same level or lower on the ranking. A Plan G enrollee can switch to another carrier's Plan G, or down to N, D, M, A, B, high-deductible G, L, or K. A Plan G enrollee cannot upgrade to Plan F or C — and because those plans are closed to new enrollees anyway, this limitation rarely matters in practice.

The more common friction is for Plan N or Plan K enrollees who would like to upgrade to Plan G. The birthday rule will not help them do so without underwriting. Their options are (1) go through medical underwriting outside the window, (2) wait for a non-birthday guaranteed-issue right (loss of coverage, carrier insolvency, trial right), or (3) remain on their current plan.

Carrier-switching within the same plan letter is always allowed: The one thing every birthday rule state agrees on is that you can switch to a different carrier's version of your current plan letter. Plan G to Plan G, Plan N to Plan N. This is where the pricing arbitrage lives, and it is the most common use of the rule.

The Three-Step Switch Workflow

Executing a birthday rule switch is straightforward if you work through the steps in order. The timing matters because applications filed outside the window will be processed under standard medical underwriting rules.

Step 1: Confirm Your Window and Get Current Quotes (Before the Window Opens)

Four to six weeks before your birthday, pull the current Medigap rates in your state. The sources that work for most people:

  • Medicare.gov Plan Finder. Select "Medicare Supplement Insurance" and enter your ZIP code. The federal site lists carriers and starting-rate ranges but does not quote you specifically.
  • Your state Department of Insurance Medigap rate guide. Most states publish an annual guide with carrier-by-carrier rates by age band. Google "[your state] Medigap rate guide" and look for the state agency link, not a broker site.
  • An independent broker. Brokers who represent multiple carriers can quote you across the market quickly. Ask explicitly for carrier-by-carrier rates on your current plan letter, and ask whether the broker has an incentive to steer you toward any specific carrier.

Make a list of the three to five carriers with the best rates on your current plan letter. Check each one for rate-increase history (your state Department of Insurance usually publishes historical rate change data) and financial strength (AM Best rating of A- or higher is the typical bar).

Step 2: Apply During the Window (Day One, If Possible)

When your window opens, apply with your top-choice carrier. Many birthday-rule applications are processed through a short-form application that confirms your current Medigap coverage and birthday rule eligibility rather than asking health questions. If the carrier's application asks health questions anyway, write "Birthday Rule — [State] [Statute Reference]" across the top and attach a copy of your current Medigap policy declarations page.

Apply as early in the window as possible. If the first carrier declines (unusual but possible if they claim your current plan does not qualify), you have time to apply with a second carrier before the window closes. If you apply on day 59 of a 60-day window and get a surprise, you have no fallback.

Request an effective date that gives you a seamless transition. Most carriers will set the new policy's effective date on the first day of the month following approval. Confirm the start date in writing before terminating your old policy.

Step 3: Terminate the Old Policy on the Right Day

Once your new policy is approved and has an effective date, call your old carrier and request termination on the day before the new policy begins. Never terminate the old policy before the new one is confirmed in writing. The one failure mode here is a gap in coverage: if there is any gap of more than 63 days between policies, the new carrier can impose a pre-existing-condition waiting period of up to six months, even on a guaranteed-issue application.

Keep documentation of both policies — the old policy's termination letter and the new policy's effective-date confirmation — for at least one year. If a claim is denied or a waiting period is improperly imposed, you will need these to appeal.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Missing the window by one day. Carriers enforce the window strictly. An application postmarked the day after the window closes will be processed under standard underwriting. Mark your calendar in advance and apply within the first two weeks of the window.
  • Assuming your state has the rule when it does not. Only the 13 states listed above (plus Washington's year-round rule and the stronger continuous-open-enrollment protections in CT, MA, ME, and NY) have the birthday rule. If you move to Texas, Arizona, Florida, or any of the 30-plus states without this protection, you lose it.
  • Trying to upgrade benefits. The rule is same-or-lesser-benefit in nearly every state. Carriers will decline an upgrade application and send it to underwriting instead. If you want to upgrade, that is a different conversation requiring a different pathway.
  • Switching to an attained-age-rated plan that looks cheap now. Three Medigap rating methods exist: community-rated (same rate at all ages), issue-age-rated (rate locked at the age you enrolled, rises only with inflation), and attained-age-rated (rate rises every year with your age). An attained-age-rated plan at age 68 can be the cheapest on the market and the most expensive by age 78. Check the rating method before switching.
  • Forgetting the Part D plan. Medigap and Part D are separate policies. Switching Medigap carriers does not affect your Part D plan. If your current Part D plan is right for you, you do not need to change it. If you want to compare 2027 standalone Part D plans at the same time, do it during AEP (October 15 through December 7).

What If You Live in a Non-Birthday-Rule State?

Most Americans live in states without a Medigap birthday rule. If that is you, three pathways still exist for guaranteed-issue switching:

  • Medigap Open Enrollment Period. Your one-time, six-month federal window after Part B becomes effective. This is the only guaranteed window most people in non-birthday-rule states ever get, which is why initial carrier choice at age 65 matters so much.
  • Trial right. If you joined Medicare Advantage for the first time (not switched from one MA plan to another) and want to leave within the first 12 months, you have a guaranteed-issue right to buy certain Medigap plans. This is a one-time trial right, not an annual one.
  • Loss of coverage. If your current Medigap carrier goes insolvent, you lose employer or union coverage, or your Medicare Advantage plan terminates service in your area, you get a federal guaranteed-issue window.

If none of these apply and you want to switch, you will go through medical underwriting. Many people with stable health pass underwriting without problems. If you have a recent cancer diagnosis, uncontrolled diabetes, severe cardiovascular disease, or similar conditions, an underwritten switch may not be viable, and sticking with your current carrier may be the only option.

What to Do If a Carrier Rejects a Valid Birthday Rule Application

Carriers do occasionally mishandle birthday rule applications — usually by routing them to standard underwriting by default. If this happens:

  1. Call the carrier's enrollment line and ask for the application to be re-processed under the state's birthday rule. Reference the state statute by name.
  2. If that fails, file a complaint with your state Department of Insurance. Most states process Medigap complaints within 15 to 30 days, and the department has authority to compel the carrier to issue the policy.
  3. In parallel, apply to a second carrier before your window closes. The complaint process is useful but slow, and you do not want to miss the window waiting for a resolution.

SHIP counselors can usually help you draft the complaint language. The service is free, and SHIP counselors have standing relationships with state Department of Insurance staff that often resolve a dispute faster than a cold-start complaint would.

Five-Minute Checklist

  1. Confirm your state has a Medigap birthday rule — check the table above and verify with your SHIP.
  2. Mark your window on your calendar (open date and close date).
  3. Four to six weeks before the window opens, pull current Medigap rates for your plan letter in your state.
  4. Identify three to five lower-priced carriers with strong rate stability and A- or better financial ratings.
  5. On day one of the window, apply with your top-choice carrier.
  6. Confirm new policy effective date in writing before terminating the old policy.
  7. Terminate the old policy effective the day before the new one begins.
  8. Keep documentation of both policies for one year.

A $50-to-$100-a-month premium drop on the same policy is a realistic outcome for someone who has been with the same carrier for five or more years. Over a 10-year horizon, that is $6,000 to $12,000, which is why this rule is worth the hour of homework it takes to execute.

Where to Find Help

Three free sources are consistently reliable:

  • State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP). Every state operates a SHIP that provides free, unbiased counseling on Medicare and Medigap decisions. Find your local office at shiphelp.org or 1-877-839-2675. SHIP counselors are not paid on commission and will not sell you a plan.
  • Your state Department of Insurance. Publishes Medigap rate guides and handles complaints when carriers mishandle birthday rule applications.
  • Medicare.gov Plan Finder. The federal site's Medicare Supplement section lists carriers and starting rates by ZIP code.

Independent brokers can also help, but they are paid commissions by the carriers whose plans they sell, and the commission structure varies by plan. If you use a broker, ask explicitly which carriers they represent and request a comparison across all carriers in your state, not only the ones that pay their highest commission.
