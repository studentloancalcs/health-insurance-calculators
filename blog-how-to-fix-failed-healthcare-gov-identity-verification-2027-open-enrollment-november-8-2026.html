How to Fix a Failed HealthCare.gov Identity Verification for 2027 Open Enrollment: The November 2026 Playbook

By HealthCalc Team

Published November 8, 2026

12 min read

Open Enrollment for the 2027 plan year is in its second week. For most households, the first obstacle is comparing plans. For a quieter group — several million Americans a year whose online application stalls before they ever reach plan selection — the first obstacle is a screen that says identity verification was not successful. You cannot pick a plan. You cannot see your subsidy. You cannot do anything until the verification clears.

This is not a sign that something is wrong with your application. It is a sign that Experian, the credit bureau HealthCare.gov uses behind the scenes to run a short identity quiz, does not have enough data to generate a quiz you can pass, or that a security freeze blocks the pull, or that a recent address or name change has not yet made it into the Experian file. All of these are common, none of them are your fault, and all of them are fixable — if you know the escalation path and start it before the December 15 deadline for January 1, 2027 coverage.

This guide covers the five reasons identity proofing fails, the three-tier escalation path to fix it, the exact list of documents the Marketplace accepts when you choose the upload route, the realistic timeline for each path relative to the December 15 deadline, and the two safety nets that preserve your January 1 effective date when the fix is going to take longer than you have.

Why Identity Verification Fails Even When Your Information Is Correct

HealthCare.gov does not check your identity against a federal database during account creation. It sends your name, date of birth, Social Security number, and current address to Experian's Remote Identity Proofing (RIDP) service, which pulls your credit file and generates a short quiz of out-of-wallet questions — a prior lender name, a street you lived on, a loan amount, a mortgage holder. Pass the quiz and you clear identity proofing. Fail it and your account enters a documents-required state.

Five scenarios cause the quiz to fail even when your information is completely correct.

  1. You have a thin or no credit file. Experian cannot generate a quiz without enough data. Young adults under 25, recent immigrants, people who have always used cash and debit, and anyone recovering from a long gap in credit history are often affected. There may be no quiz generated at all, in which case the system returns a failure code without ever showing you questions.
  2. You have a security freeze on your Experian credit file. A freeze blocks the credit pull entirely. The system treats a blocked pull the same as a failed quiz. This is the single most common reason for failure among consumers who followed common-sense advice to freeze their credit after a data breach.
  3. You moved, married, or divorced recently. Experian's file may still show a previous address or a prior legal name. When the quiz is based on the old file and your application uses your new details, the automated matching fails.
  4. You have an active fraud alert. Fraud alerts require additional verification that Experian cannot complete through an automated quiz. A failed fraud-alert check returns the same generic failure message you would see for any other reason.
  5. Your answers are off because the questions reach back far enough. A loan from 15 years ago, an old mortgage, an address from the 1990s — most people cannot recall these precisely. Two wrong answers out of four is enough to fail.
The Marketplace does not tell you which scenario applied. The failure message is generic. You have to work the escalation path without knowing exactly why the automated system rejected you, which is why starting all three tiers in parallel can save days when the deadline is close.

The Three-Tier Escalation Path

There are three recognized paths to clear a failed identity proofing attempt on HealthCare.gov. Each has a different turnaround, a different success rate for different underlying problems, and a different workload on your end. The decision is not which one to pick; the decision is what order to run them in given the time you have.

TierMethodBest forRealistic turnaround (during OEP)
1Retry Experian with a temporary freeze liftSecurity freeze, old address on file, outdated name1 to 3 days
2Upload identity documents through Marketplace portalThin or no credit file, repeated quiz failures, fraud alert10 to 20 days
3Phone verification with the Marketplace Call CenterAny failure, especially urgent cases and complex namesSame day (one phone call)

If you have more than three weeks before December 15, start with Tier 1 (freeze lift, if applicable) and move to Tier 2 or Tier 3 if that fails. If you have less than three weeks, skip straight to Tier 3 (phone verification) and reserve document upload as a backup.

Tier 1: Fix Your Experian File and Retry

If your identity proofing failure is due to a security freeze, an old address on your Experian file, or a thin quiz you simply got wrong, fixing the underlying Experian issue and retrying is the fastest path. It is also the only path that avoids any manual document handling on the Marketplace side.

1 Request a temporary freeze lift at Experian. Log in at experian.com or call 1-888-397-3742 and ask for a temporary lift on your security freeze. Set the start date for today and the end date three to seven days out. The freeze reinstates automatically at the end of the lift window; you do not have to remember to turn it back on.

2 Update any outdated information on your Experian file. If your address, name, or employer in Experian's records is out of date, submit a correction through Experian's dispute process. A corrected address usually propagates within three to five business days, which is enough time to still clear identity proofing before December 15 if you start in early November.

3 Wait 24 hours, then retry the quiz on HealthCare.gov. Log in with your Marketplace account and look for the option to re-try identity verification. Answer the new quiz. If you pass, you can continue the enrollment immediately. If you fail again — even once the freeze is lifted and the file is correct — stop retrying and move to Tier 2 or Tier 3. Repeated failed attempts can lock your account for 24 hours.

If you lost your Experian PIN, you will need a replacement before you can lift the freeze. Submit a PIN replacement request at 1-888-397-3742 or by mail with a copy of your government ID; Experian issues a new PIN by mail within seven to ten business days. That delay is significant against the December 15 deadline, so if you are inside two weeks, skip Tier 1 and move to Tier 3 (phone verification) instead.

Tier 2: Upload Identity Documents

If the Experian path is not going to work or you have already tried it, uploading identity documents to the Marketplace is the next step. This is the route most consumers end up using, and it works for every failure scenario, but it is also the slowest.

Documents the Marketplace Accepts

The Marketplace accepts a combination from two categories.

Category A — One document showing name and photo. The strongest option; one is sufficient.

  • Driver's license or state-issued ID card
  • U.S. passport or passport card
  • Federally recognized Tribal enrollment card or photo ID
  • Military ID card
  • Permanent Resident Card (green card)
  • Employment Authorization Card
  • Certificate of Naturalization (N-550 or N-570) or Certificate of Citizenship (N-560 or N-561)
  • Document from a federal, state, or local agency showing name and photo

Category B — Documents showing name and date of birth. Submit two from this list if no Category A option is available.

  • Birth certificate (long form; a hospital certificate of birth is not sufficient on its own)
  • Adoption decree
  • Hospital or clinic record signed by a physician or midwife
  • Social Security card paired with proof of age
  • School ID or school record for a dependent
  • Certified copy of a court record showing name and date of birth

How to Upload

1 Log in at HealthCare.gov and go to your application. You should see a to-do list item for identity verification. Click the document upload link.

2 Take a clear photo or scan of each document. Make sure every corner is visible, text is readable, and nothing is cropped off. Save as JPG, PNG, PDF, GIF, TIFF, or BMP. Each file must be under 10 MB.

3 Upload each file through the portal and label it correctly (the system prompts for the document type). Submit.

4 Note your upload confirmation number. Check back on your Marketplace to-do list every three to five days.

The Marketplace Verification Center reviews uploaded documents within about 10 business days outside Open Enrollment. During Open Enrollment the queue stretches; expect 15 to 20 days from mid-November onward, longer the closer you get to December 15. If you are approaching the deadline, upload documents for a paper trail and call the Call Center for faster manual verification in parallel.

Tier 3: Phone Verification (Fastest Path)

The Marketplace Call Center can complete identity verification manually by phone in a single call, without any documents required. This is the fastest and most reliable escalation path, and it is what we recommend whenever time is short.

1 Call 1-800-318-2596 (TTY 1-855-889-4325). The Call Center is open 24 hours a day, seven days a week, excluding federal holidays. Have your Marketplace application ID or reference number ready.

2 Tell the representative that your online identity proofing failed and that you need manual identity verification. They will transfer you to a specialized agent authorized to complete manual verification.

3 Answer the manual verification questions. The specialized agent pulls from government and administrative data sources distinct from Experian (Social Security Administration records, IRS records, prior Marketplace enrollment history). Questions may include prior addresses going back five years, prior employers, the name of a parent or sibling, and other facts drawn from federal records.

4 Record the agent name and call reference number before you hang up. If manual verification clears you, continue your enrollment online within 24 hours. Your account will be in a cleared state for plan selection.

Call Center hold times are predictable. Early morning (6–9 AM local time) and late evening are the shortest waits. The week of November 10 through November 21 is the calmest period of Open Enrollment. The week of December 10 through December 15 sees the longest holds — often 45 to 90 minutes — and the specialized manual verification queue is slower still. Call now rather than in December if you can.

If the Fix Is Going to Take Longer Than December 15

If you started the process in early November, all three tiers fit comfortably before the December 15 deadline for a January 1, 2027 effective date. If you are starting mid-December and the documents route is going to take 15 to 20 days, two safety nets preserve some of your coverage.

Safety Net 1: February 1 Effective Date

Identity verification that clears between December 16 and January 15, 2027 lets you enroll under the standard Open Enrollment window, but your effective date becomes February 1 rather than January 1. You lose January coverage, which means any care you receive in January is at cash prices. If the gap is unavoidable, use the time to call providers and ask about cash pay rates, which are often lower than the billed price if you ask up front.

Safety Net 2: Exceptional Circumstances SEP

If identity verification is still in process when January 15 passes, call the Marketplace Call Center and request a Complex Case Form (Form 10178) or a similar exceptional-circumstances escalation. Explain that your application was submitted during Open Enrollment, that identity proofing is in progress through no fault of your own, and that the delay is outside your control. If granted, your effective date can be backdated to January 1, 2027, and your APTC applies from the first of the year.

The documentation you want for this escalation is simple: upload confirmation numbers and dates, call reference numbers, the date of your original application submission, and any correspondence from the Verification Center. Keep all of these from day one. If you end up not needing them, nothing is lost. If you do, the paper trail is the difference between a January 1 backdate and a February 1 effective date.

Three Mistakes That Make a Fixable Problem Worse

  1. Retrying the Experian quiz too many times. After three failed attempts, HealthCare.gov locks identity proofing for 24 hours. Repeated failures also create noise on your account that the Call Center has to clear before manual verification can proceed. If the first quiz fails and a freeze lift does not resolve it, stop retrying and move to Tier 2 or Tier 3.
  2. Treating identity verification as the subsidy calculation. It is not. Your subsidy is calculated from your 2027 MAGI and household size on the application itself. Identity verification gates plan selection, not subsidy eligibility. Submitting the application early (even before identity verification clears) locks in your projected subsidy calculation and establishes your paper-trail start date for any later escalation.
  3. Waiting until December 10 to start. The compounding effect of Call Center hold times, Verification Center queue times, and Experian processing delays makes early December the worst time to begin fixing a failed verification. Starting in early November gives every tier breathing room. Starting mid-December forces you into Tier 3 as your only option and into a 90-minute hold.

Bottom line: A failed identity verification on HealthCare.gov is not a rejection of your application and is not about your credit score. It is Experian's Remote Identity Proofing system saying it cannot generate or score a quiz for you, almost always for a reason that has nothing to do with your actual identity. Three paths fix it: lift your Experian freeze and retry, upload identity documents, or call the Marketplace Call Center for manual verification. Start in early November, not December, and run the paths in parallel if the deadline is close. If the fix spills past December 15, document every submission and ask for a Complex Case Form if you end up needing a January 1 backdate.
