How to Switch ACA Plans When Your 2027 Insurer Exits Your County: The November 2026 Protocol

By HealthCalc Team

Published November 4, 2026

13 min read

Open Enrollment for the 2027 plan year opened Sunday, November 1, 2026. For most households, the first week has been a question of comparing options. For roughly 700,000 households — the enrollees affected by twelve major insurer exits from state Marketplaces — the question is sharper. Your plan is not going to exist on January 1. The letter in the mail said so.

The twelve exits, as of early November 2026, are not a single storyline. Cigna is the largest single footprint change, leaving all eleven states where it currently sells plans, affecting roughly 369,000 members. Baylor Scott & White is leaving the Texas exchange, taking about 100,000 enrollees with it. Molina Healthcare is cutting its ACA Marketplace footprint from 14 states to six. Centene's Sunshine State Health Plan is leaving Florida (with members transitioning to another Centene subsidiary that still offers Ambetter plans there). Centene is also leaving Delaware and New Hampshire exchange coverage. UnitedHealthcare is exiting Georgia. Medica is dropping Iowa, Kansas, and Oklahoma. ConnectiCare is leaving the Connecticut exchange. Cox HealthPlans is leaving Missouri. CareSource is leaving Indiana, West Virginia, and Ohio. PacificSource is exiting Oregon, Idaho, and Montana. Providence Health Plan is winding down most business including its Oregon ACA plans. And Mending (formerly Taro Health) is exiting health insurance entirely, affecting Maine and Oklahoma.

If you are in that group, the next six weeks matter in a way they do not for a household whose carrier is staying. This guide covers what the insurer exit letter actually means, how auto-reenrollment behaves differently depending on your state, the step-by-step protocol to pick a replacement plan without losing your subsidy or your doctor, the loss-of-coverage Special Enrollment Period that extends your deadline to early March if everything goes wrong, and the three mistakes that produce the worst outcomes.

What the Insurer Exit Letter Actually Says

Every carrier leaving the ACA Marketplace for 2027 is required to send a written discontinuation notice to each affected enrollee at least 90 days before the plan terminates. For plans ending December 31, 2026, that means notices went out in late September and October 2026. If you have not received yours by now and you suspect your carrier is on the exit list, check your HealthCare.gov inbox, your carrier's member portal, and your paper mail from the past eight weeks. The notice is a legal document and your proof of the qualifying event; the loss-of-coverage Special Enrollment Period hinges on it.

A standard insurer exit letter contains six elements. The 2026 plan name and policy number you currently hold. The plan termination date (almost always December 31, 2026 for Marketplace plans). An explicit statement that this plan will not be offered for 2027. Information about auto-reenrollment behavior in your state. Often, a specific replacement plan the exchange or carrier has pre-selected for you based on metal level, network type, and premium similarity. And a deadline to pick a new plan without a coverage gap — typically December 15, 2026 for a January 1, 2027 effective date.

Do not accept the pre-selected replacement as a default. The exchange's auto-selection logic optimizes for continuity of metal level, network type, and low premium disruption. It does not check whether your doctors are in the new network, whether your prescriptions are on the new formulary, or whether your deductible and out-of-pocket maximum match your expected utilization. Use the pre-selected plan as a starting candidate only, never as the automatic choice.

Save the paper letter. Take a photo of it. Store it with your tax documents, in a folder labeled with the plan year. You will need it if you end up relying on the loss-of-coverage SEP in January or February, and you will need it if you later appeal a Marketplace decision about your effective date or subsidy.

Auto-Reenrollment Behaves Differently By State

HealthCare.gov and the state-run exchanges handle an insurer exit in one of three ways. Knowing which one applies to you decides whether doing nothing produces coverage on January 1 or a gap.

Exchange typeAuto-reenrollment rule when insurer exitsWhat happens if you do nothing
HealthCare.gov (36 states + DC)Auto-selects lowest-cost plan with same metal level, product type, and network type among remaining carriersYou are enrolled in a replacement January 1, often at a different premium and formulary
Most state-run exchangesSimilar logic to HealthCare.gov, but specific rules varyYou are enrolled in a replacement, but verify before assuming
Colorado and a handful of others (for specific exits)No auto-reenrollment when original carrier exits; enrollees must actively pick a new planYou are uninsured on January 1
Off-exchange coverage, any stateNo auto-reenrollment under any circumstancesYou are uninsured on January 1

The Colorado rule for Cigna enrollees is the clearest cautionary example. Colorado's state-run exchange, Connect for Health Colorado, has stated that the roughly 100,000 Coloradans enrolled in Cigna plans will not be automatically transferred to a new carrier for 2027. Each has to log in, pick a replacement plan, and submit it. If they do not, they are uninsured on January 1, 2027, with no automatic retroactive fix. Enrollees in other states where Cigna is exiting (Arizona, Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Indiana, Mississippi, North Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia) will see standard HealthCare.gov auto-reenrollment behavior, though the specific plan the exchange picks may not match individual circumstances.

If you do not know which exchange your state uses, go to HealthCare.gov and start a new application; if it redirects you to a state-specific URL, you are in a state-run exchange. Call that exchange directly and ask: "What is your auto-reenrollment policy when my current carrier exits for 2027?" Record the answer. If the answer is unclear or depends on the specific exit, assume no auto-reenrollment and pick a plan yourself.

The Six-Step Replacement Plan Protocol

Run this on every household that lost coverage because of an insurer exit. The time commitment is 60 to 90 minutes if nothing goes sideways, longer if your current doctors or drugs sit on the edge of what the new plans cover.

1Update your 2027 MAGI estimate first

Log in to HealthCare.gov or your state exchange and update your income estimate for the 2027 plan year. The subsidy you saw in 2026 is not the subsidy you will get in 2027 — the enhanced premium tax credits that expired at the end of 2025 are not extended for plan year 2027, which reintroduces the 400 percent FPL cliff and shifts the applicable-percentage schedule back to the pre-2021 regime. A bad income estimate produces a bad net-premium number, and a plan picked on a bad net-premium number is a plan picked blind. Enter your best estimate of 2027 household MAGI, confirm or update household members, and let the system recalculate.

2Build a two-column short list: metal level and network type

If you held a Silver plan in 2026 with a PPO or EPO network, the first pass is to limit 2027 options to the same metal level and the same or compatible network type. If your 2026 plan was HSA-eligible, limit the 2027 short list to HSA-eligible plans, which means Bronze or qualified HDHPs. Record the three to five candidates that come through the first filter. Do not expand yet.

3Verify provider network for each candidate

For every doctor you see at least twice a year, run the provider lookup on each candidate plan. Confirm by phone with the billing office (not the receptionist) that the specific 2027 plan name and HIOS ID is in network. The directory is not reliable on its own; our prior guide on spotting a ghost network in a 2027 ACA plan covers the common failure modes. If a specialist is not in the new network and you are in active treatment, note the plan but do not disqualify it yet; continuity-of-care provisions (below) may let you keep that provider for 60 to 90 days.

4Verify formulary match for each candidate

Pull the 2027 formulary document or online lookup for each candidate plan. Check every prescription anyone in the household takes, including occasional ones (migraine abortives, controller inhalers, ADHD stimulants on an as-needed schedule). Record the tier, utilization management flags (PA for prior authorization, ST for step therapy, QL for quantity limits), and preferred-pharmacy indicator. Our step-by-step prescription formulary matching guide walks through the verification protocol in detail. A drug sitting on Tier 5 with 40 percent coinsurance under one plan and Tier 2 with a flat copay under another can swing annual cost by thousands.

5Translate every candidate into a total projected 2027 cost

For each candidate that survives Steps 3 and 4, calculate: annual premium (post-subsidy) + expected annual drug cost + expected annual non-drug utilization (routine care, planned procedures, specialists). The lowest premium is almost never the lowest total cost. Our ACA Subsidy Calculator and Plan Cost Calculator handle the premium side; the Drug Cost Finder handles the formulary side. Run each candidate through both to produce a single annual-cost number per plan.

6Pick before December 15, 2026

The hard deadline for a January 1, 2027 effective date is December 15, 2026. Enroll on or before that date through HealthCare.gov or your state exchange and your new coverage begins when your old plan ends, with no gap. Pick the candidate with the lowest total projected cost that passes the network and formulary filters. Save confirmation of enrollment. Do not wait for the carrier to send a welcome packet before confirming effective dates through the exchange.

Continuity of Care for In-Progress Treatment

If you are in the middle of a course of treatment — a planned surgery, cancer therapy, pregnancy, mental-health treatment on a specific provider, physical therapy on a specific prescription — and the new plan's network does not include your current provider, continuity-of-care protections may let you keep that provider for a transition period at in-network rates.

Continuity of care is governed by a mix of federal ACA regulations and state insurance law, and the specifics vary by state and plan type. The common framework: you request a transition-of-care authorization from the new carrier within 30 days of coverage starting (earlier is better), documenting the active course of treatment and medical necessity of continuing with the current provider. If approved, you can continue with the out-of-network provider at in-network cost sharing for a defined period, typically 60 to 90 days, sometimes extended for pregnancy through delivery and postpartum. The current provider may need to agree to accept the new plan's in-network payment rate during the transition period; some do, some do not.

The request must be timely. Submitting it in April for coverage that started January 1 is usually denied. Submit it with the first premium payment, in writing, with a one-paragraph summary of the treatment and medical necessity signed by the treating physician. Save every letter and confirmation; if denied, the plan is required to provide an appeal path.

The Loss-of-Coverage Special Enrollment Period

If anything goes wrong in the ordinary enrollment path — you missed December 15 and ended up in auto-reenrollment at the wrong plan, you discovered a formulary mismatch in the second week of January, your network verification was based on a bad directory and you learned in February that your doctor is not in the new network — the loss-of-coverage Special Enrollment Period is the safety net.

The SEP rules: involuntary loss of minimum essential coverage, including your carrier exiting the market, triggers a 60-day window. The window starts 60 days before the plan ends and runs 60 days after. For a plan that ends December 31, 2026, the SEP window runs from November 1, 2026 through March 1, 2027. During the SEP you can enroll in or switch to any available Marketplace plan without needing another qualifying event.

The practical use of the SEP: You should still pick a plan by December 15 for a January 1 effective date. The SEP is insurance against mistakes in the ordinary path, not a substitute for it. If you discover in late January that the plan you picked does not actually cover a critical specialist, you can switch during the SEP, with a February 1 or March 1 effective date depending on when you file. Coverage for the first few weeks of January under the original wrong plan still counts as coverage (so you are not uninsured), and you avoid the full-year lock-in that applies after January 15 for enrollees who did not experience a qualifying event.

Keep the insurer exit letter. Keep the HealthCare.gov confirmation of your original plan selection. Keep any written communication from the new carrier. These three documents, together, establish the qualifying event and your eligibility for the SEP. Apply through HealthCare.gov or your state exchange; select "loss of health coverage" as the qualifying event and upload the exit letter when prompted.

Three Mistakes That Produce the Worst Outcomes

Mistake 1: Accepting the exchange's auto-selected replacement without checking

The auto-selection logic matches on metal level, network type, and low premium. It does not check whether your doctors are in the new network or whether your drugs are on the new formulary. Enrollees who accept the auto-selection without verification routinely discover in January that their established cardiologist is not in network, their asthma controller moved from Tier 2 to Tier 4, or their specialty biologic is excluded entirely. By then the Open Enrollment window has narrowed, auto-selection is already in force, and the correction requires a switch during the SEP.

Mistake 2: Assuming the subsidy carries over unchanged

The enhanced premium tax credits that lowered premiums for 2021 through 2025 enrollees expired at the end of 2025. For plan year 2027, the pre-2021 applicable-percentage schedule applies, which raises the share of income enrollees are expected to pay for a benchmark Silver plan and reinstates the 400 percent FPL subsidy cliff. If you were between 400 percent and 600 percent of FPL and benefited from the enhanced subsidies in recent years, your 2027 subsidy drops to zero. If you were below 400 percent of FPL, your subsidy likely still exists but at a different dollar amount. The subsidy number on your HealthCare.gov profile at the start of November reflects your 2026 eligibility, not 2027. Update your income estimate first, then compare net premiums. Our prior guide on 2027 ACA benchmark premiums and the returning subsidy cliff walks through the math at three worked income brackets.

Mistake 3: Letting coverage lapse into a cash-pay gap

Picking a plan after December 15 produces a February 1 effective date in most states, which creates a January gap where any care you receive is at cash prices. A brand or specialty drug refilled cash in January can cost $1,000 to $5,000 for a single month. A planned procedure moved into the gap by an admin delay becomes a cash-pay medical event. The December 15 deadline is not about the Open Enrollment calendar; it is about the gap. If you cannot complete the full six-step protocol by December 15, pick the best candidate you have on December 14, and plan to switch during the SEP if the first choice turns out to be wrong. A suboptimal plan with coverage beats an optimal plan without.
