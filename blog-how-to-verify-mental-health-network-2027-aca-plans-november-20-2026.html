How to Verify Mental Health and Behavioral Health Network Access on 2027 ACA Plans Before December 15 — The Ghost-Therapist Trap

By HealthCalc Team

Published November 20, 2026

13 min read

Your 2027 ACA plan preview shows 412 in-network therapists within 15 miles of your zip code. Reality, according to a decade of secret-shopper studies: somewhere between 165 and 250 of those listings are inaccurate, and of the remainder, fewer than half are actually accepting new patients. The real number of therapists you can book an appointment with in the first 60 days of a new plan is often one-tenth of the directory total. Behavioral health has by a wide margin the highest “ghost provider” rate of any specialty on ACA Marketplace plans, and that rate did not materially improve between 2020 and 2026 despite tightened CMS rules.

The practical consequence is that a 2027 plan which looks adequate on paper — big directory, familiar carrier, parity-compliant SBC — can leave an enrollee calling 20 or 30 therapists over three weeks before finding one who answers, takes the plan, is in-network for 2027 (not just 2026), and has openings. Eight weeks of lost appointments at the start of the plan year is the usual outcome. For enrollees mid-treatment, that gap often means stopping therapy. For new enrollees, it often means never starting.

This guide walks through what the ghost-therapist problem actually looks like on 2027 plans, which carriers and plan designs are worst affected, the verification protocol that reduces ghost listings from 60 percent to near zero before you enroll, what the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA) gives you if the network is inadequate, and the three enrollment mistakes that cost 2027 enrollees access to therapy. You have until December 15, 2026 to pick a plan for January 1, 2027 coverage, and the verification work takes about 45 to 90 minutes per plan on your short list.

What a ghost therapist actually is

A ghost therapist is a behavioral health provider whose listing in a plan's directory does not match reality. The specific mismatches, in roughly descending order of frequency:

  • Not accepting new patients. The most common failure. The directory says the therapist is in-network; the office says the waitlist is six to twelve months.
  • No longer accepting this plan. The therapist dropped the carrier or the specific plan's network after a reimbursement cut, but the carrier never removed the listing. Common when a plan switches from one network to a narrower sub-network for 2027.
  • Wrong address or phone number. The therapist moved, retired, or changed group affiliation. Phone number reaches a disconnected line or a different practice.
  • Specialty mismatch. The directory lists the provider as treating a condition (OCD, eating disorders, trauma, ADHD, autism) that the provider does not actually treat.
  • License type mismatch. The directory lists the provider as a psychiatrist when they are a psychiatric nurse practitioner, or as a psychologist when they are a licensed clinical social worker. Not fatal, but it misstates the services the provider can bill (medication management, psychological testing).
  • Virtual-only when directory implies in-person. Increasingly common with telehealth-focused group practices. Not necessarily a problem if you want virtual care, but a surprise if you want in-person.
  • Provider does not exist. A small share of listings reflect credentialing errors or old records; the provider named is no longer licensed in the state, never was licensed, or never joined the practice listed.
Key idea: The directory tells you a provider was once in the plan's network. It does not tell you whether that provider is in-network right now, taking this plan right now, accepting new patients right now, reachable right now, or qualified for your reason for seeking care right now. Verification closes those seven gaps.

Which 2027 plans and markets are most affected

Behavioral health network adequacy varies enormously by state, carrier, and plan design. The patterns that matter for 2027 enrollment, drawn from 2026 state insurance-department secret-shopper audits and carrier filings:

  • Bronze and lower-premium Silver plans. The narrower the network and the lower the reimbursement, the worse the ghost rate. 2027 Bronze plans frequently show ghost rates above 55 percent for behavioral health.
  • Narrow and tiered networks. Plans marketed as “Preferred,” “Select,” “Essential,” “Classic,” or “Value” HMOs and EPOs tend to have smaller behavioral health lists with higher ghost rates. Pair this with our hospital tier verification guide because tier logic often applies to behavioral health facilities too.
  • Rural counties. Fewer therapists overall, so a small number of ghost listings crowds out most of the real network. Twenty counties in Texas, 14 in Arizona, 10 in Georgia, and dozens across the Mountain West have fewer than five in-network therapists accepting new patients across any 2027 ACA plan.
  • Specialty-sensitive care. OCD specialists, eating disorder specialists, trauma specialists trained in EMDR or CPT, child and adolescent psychiatrists, autism-spectrum specialists, and psychiatrists who treat ADHD in adults are the subsegments with the worst ghost-provider gaps. Many specialists in these areas have left commercial insurance entirely for cash-pay and concierge models.
  • Carriers with recent network reorganizations. If a carrier changed its 2027 provider network significantly (new sub-networks, new reimbursement contract, a carrier exit from a county that pushed enrollees into a new carrier), the directory is more likely to be out of date in January 2027.

Carriers consistently rated better for behavioral health network accuracy in 2026 state audits include Kaiser Permanente (integrated model), the largest Blue Cross Blue Shield plans in each state, and Oscar in metros where it operates its own in-house behavioral health service. Carriers consistently rated worse include several national Medicare Advantage carriers that also operate ACA plans, carriers recently acquired, and startup ACA carriers with small market share. State audit reports are published annually by most state insurance departments and are worth skimming for your state before you enroll.

The six-step behavioral health verification protocol

Run this on every plan on your short list before December 15. Expect 45 to 90 minutes per plan. Keep a running notebook of names, phone numbers, dates, and reference numbers.

1Pull three therapist lists from the plan preview

On HealthCare.gov or your state exchange, open the plan preview tool and for each short-listed plan pull three separate directory lists: (a) licensed therapists (LCSW, LMFT, LPC) within 15 miles of your zip code, (b) psychologists (PhD or PsyD) within the same radius, and (c) psychiatrists and psychiatric nurse practitioners within 30 miles. Save each list as a text file, screenshot, or print-to-PDF. You want the names, NPI numbers, and phone numbers for the next step. Prefer the carrier's own directory to the HealthCare.gov view, because HealthCare.gov sometimes pulls stale data from the carrier's prior-year API feed.

2Call ten therapists per license type to measure the real-access rate

Pick ten names at random from each list. Call each one. Ask three questions and note the answer: “Are you in-network with [carrier] [plan name and HIOS plan ID] for the 2027 plan year effective January 1, 2027? Are you accepting new patients? What is your first available intake appointment?” This is your ghost-network stress test. If 7 of 10 calls go to voicemail, wrong numbers, or get a “not accepting new patients” answer, your real network is roughly 30 percent of the directory. If the first-available appointment is more than 60 days out for most of the ones who accept new patients, your practical access is further restricted.

This is the single step that separates a workable plan from an unworkable one. A plan that passes this test well is worth prioritizing even if its premium is higher. A plan that fails this test is a plan with nominal behavioral health coverage and no practical access.

3Verify specialty match for any specific needs

If you are seeking care for a specific condition (OCD, eating disorders, trauma/PTSD, perinatal mental health, child and adolescent, autism-spectrum, substance use), screen each therapist on your list for whether they actually treat that condition and use evidence-based protocols (ERP for OCD, CBT-E or FBT for eating disorders, EMDR or CPT for trauma). Directory specialty tags are not reliable. Ask on the intake call: “Do you treat [condition]? What approach do you use? How many patients with [condition] have you worked with in the past year?”

4Check the plan's virtual behavioral health benefit

Almost every 2027 ACA plan includes at least one virtual-care partner for behavioral health. Talkspace, MDLive, Teladoc, Doctor on Demand, Brightline (pediatric), and carrier-owned platforms are the most common. Confirm which virtual platform your plan covers, the copay or coinsurance for virtual behavioral health visits, whether the platform supports both therapy and psychiatric medication management, and whether there is a session cap. Virtual access is typically much faster than brick-and-mortar — same-day or next-week intake is common — and it functions as a backstop when the in-person network is thin. This is especially useful when a provider you want is in-network with the carrier but only via telehealth. Pair this check with our 2027 ACA telehealth coverage guide.

5Read the SBC and plan documents for parity and visit caps

Open the plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage and look for the “Mental/Behavioral Health Outpatient Services” and “Mental/Behavioral Health Inpatient Services” rows. Compare the cost-sharing to the general outpatient and inpatient rows. Under MHPAEA, they must be comparable; a plan that charges a higher copay or applies a visit cap only to behavioral health is in violation. Also check the Evidence of Coverage for preauthorization requirements for outpatient therapy. A plan that requires preauthorization or a step-therapy equivalent for routine outpatient therapy is another red flag worth reporting to the state insurance department.

6Call member services to confirm plan-year 2027 network credentialing

Before enrolling, call the carrier's member services line (not the sales line) and ask: “Can you verify that [therapist name and NPI] is credentialed and in-network for [plan name and HIOS plan ID] for the 2027 plan year effective January 1, 2027?” Get a case or reference number for the call. A rep who hesitates, says “you should check the directory,” or cannot confirm the therapist's 2027 status is itself diagnostic: the plan's internal systems do not have confirmed 2027 network data, and the directory listing cannot be trusted.

What to do when the network is inadequate

Option 1: Pick a different plan where the network holds up

Run the step 2 call test on the next plan on your short list. Many markets have one or two ACA plans with meaningfully better behavioral health access than the rest, and the premium difference is often $20 to $60 per month after subsidies. Model the total expected cost (premium + therapy copays + out-of-pocket spend) with the Plan Cost Calculator and the ACA Subsidy Calculator to see whether the premium gap is worth it.

Option 2: File a Network Access Exception after enrollment

A Network Access Exception (also called an In-Network Benefit Request or Benefit Level Exception) asks the plan to pay an out-of-network therapist at in-network cost-sharing because the in-network network is inadequate. Grounds the plan is required to consider under MHPAEA: no in-network therapist within reasonable time and distance, no in-network therapist accepting new patients, no in-network therapist with the specialty you need, no in-network therapist with appointments within four weeks. File the request on the plan's exception form once your 2027 coverage is active, with the list of 10+ therapists you called and their responses attached. Decisions are typically due within 15 business days for non-urgent requests and 72 hours for urgent requests.

Option 3: Use virtual behavioral health as your primary access route

For many enrollees, the fastest and most reliable 2027 behavioral health access is the plan's contracted virtual platform. If the virtual benefit is comparable to the in-person benefit on cost-sharing, and if virtual care fits your clinical needs, treat the virtual benefit as the primary access and use in-person only when necessary. This is a legitimate solution, not a workaround.

Option 4: File a Mental Health Parity complaint

If the plan refuses a Network Access Exception and the network is clearly inadequate, file a parity complaint with your state insurance department and, for employer plans, with the federal Department of Labor's Employee Benefits Security Administration (EBSA). Parity complaints are investigated under the 2024 final regulations and the 2026 updates, and the median resolution time is 30 to 60 days. Many complaints end with the plan approving an exception, expanding the network, or paying for out-of-network therapy at in-network cost-sharing. Our mental health parity appeals guide covers the complaint mechanics in detail.

Three enrollment mistakes that cost 2027 enrollees access to therapy

Mistake 1: Trusting the directory count

A big number of listed therapists does not mean a workable network. The 412-provider directory is often the same 50 real providers plus several hundred stale listings. The call test is the only signal that matters. Treat directory counts as a ceiling, not a floor.

Mistake 2: Enrolling now and “sorting it out in January”

January 1 is when ten million people activate new plans and start calling therapists. The in-network intake slots fill in the first two weeks. If you wait until January to run the verification protocol, you will be behind the curve and the real network will look even thinner than it does in November. Do the work now.

Mistake 3: Ignoring the virtual benefit until you need it

Virtual behavioral health on 2027 plans is usually the fastest and most reliable path to a therapist. Enrollees who dismiss it as inferior often end up relying on it when the in-person network fails and then struggle because they did not verify which platform their plan covers. Learn the virtual benefit at enrollment, not in a crisis.

Special considerations

Child and adolescent behavioral health

The ghost-provider rate is highest for child and adolescent psychiatry. Many 2027 plans cover Brightline or similar pediatric behavioral health platforms at low cost-sharing; if your plan does, that may be the most reliable route for a child under 18.

Substance use disorder treatment

SUD treatment (intensive outpatient programs, medication-assisted treatment, residential) is covered under MHPAEA on parity with medical/surgical benefits. Verify the specific facility or program, not just a counselor. Preauthorization is common for IOPs and residential.

HSA, FSA, and HRA uses

Therapy copays and cash-pay therapy for a diagnosed condition are HSA- and FSA-eligible expenses. A plan with a weak behavioral health network and a strong HSA option can be workable if the enrollee is prepared to cash-pay a therapist and reimburse through the HSA. Our HSA for therapy guide covers eligibility and documentation.

Mid-treatment transitions from 2026 coverage

If you are currently in therapy on 2026 coverage and your 2027 plan will not include that therapist in-network, file a Transition of Care request on day one of the 2027 plan to keep your current therapist at in-network cost-sharing for 90 days. See our Transition of Care request guide for the filing protocol.
