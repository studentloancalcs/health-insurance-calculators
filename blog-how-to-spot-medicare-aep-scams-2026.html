How to Spot Medicare AEP Scams in 2026: AI Voice Clones, Fake $2,100 Cap 'Activation' Calls, and the Red Flags That Give Scammers Away

By HealthCalc Team

Published October 8, 2026

10 min read

The Annual Election Period (AEP) opens October 15 and closes December 7, and in that eight-week window about 65 million Medicare beneficiaries decide whether to keep their Advantage plan, swap Part D coverage, or move back to Original Medicare for 2027. Scammers know the calendar as well as CMS does. The call volume targeting seniors starts climbing in late September and peaks the first week of November, and the 2026 wave is harder to spot than any year before it — because the people on the other end of the line now sound like your daughter, your pharmacist, or a Medicare representative reading a script that sounds exactly like a real one.

This guide walks through the four scams running right now, the CMS marketing rules that real agents must follow (and that scammers always break), a phone-call checklist that works even when the caller sounds convincing, and what to do in the next 24 hours if you think you already gave a scammer your Medicare number.

The One Rule That Catches Almost Every Scam

Memorize this and the rest of the article is easier: Medicare does not call you. Not during AEP, not out of the blue, not to issue a new card, not to activate a benefit, not to confirm your information. Medicare communicates with beneficiaries through the mail (the Medicare & You handbook each fall, the ANOC and Evidence of Coverage from your plan by September 30), through secure portals you log into at MyMedicare.gov, and through return calls to 1-800-MEDICARE when you dial that number first.

If an incoming call claims to be Medicare and you did not initiate it, hang up. That single rule prevents the majority of what follows. The reason the rest of this article matters is that the scam calls have become good enough that beneficiaries hesitate before hanging up — and in that hesitation, they get worked.

The 2026 verification habit: If any call is urgent, hang up and dial the number on the back of your Medicare card or the number on your plan's membership card. The call that was real will still be waiting for you when you call back. The scam will not.

Scam 1: The AI Voice-Clone Call

The fastest-growing Medicare scam of 2026 is the AI voice-clone call. Criminals use short audio clips harvested from social media posts, voicemail greetings, podcast appearances, or recorded calls to generate a cloned voice of a family member, friend, pharmacist, insurance agent, or doctor. The cloned voice then calls the beneficiary and asks for Medicare information, a payment, or a "plan upgrade" confirmation.

What the call sounds like

  • "Mom, it's me — I'm at the pharmacy and they said your Medicare number isn't going through. Can you read it to me?"
  • "This is Dr. [name]'s office following up on your prescription. We need to re-verify your Part D plan ID before we can send it in."
  • "Hi, this is [name] from the Medicare office — we met at the senior center last year. I'm calling about a 2027 benefit change you qualify for."

Why it works

A convincing voice short-circuits the usual skepticism. The caller doesn't need to persuade the beneficiary that Medicare is calling — they only need to persuade them that someone they already trust is calling.

How to defuse it

  1. If the voice sounds like someone you know, hang up and call that person back on a number you already have.
  2. If the voice claims to be from a provider, hang up and call the provider's main number from your card or statement.
  3. Agree in advance with family members on a shared password or question (a city you both know, a pet's name) that the caller must say first.
Do not rely on caller ID. Caller ID spoofing is trivial. A call that shows "SSA," "MEDICARE," or your doctor's office name in the display window can still be a scammer. The only reliable verification is dialing a number you already trust.

Scam 2: The Fake "$2,100 Drug-Cap Activation" Call

The Inflation Reduction Act created a $2,100 out-of-pocket cap for Part D prescription drugs that took effect January 1, 2026. The cap applies automatically to every Part D plan — standalone PDPs and Medicare Advantage plans with drug coverage. There is no application, no enrollment, no fee, no "activation."

The scam call tells the beneficiary that they need to "activate" or "register for" the cap, usually asking for a processing fee ($39.99 is a popular number) or the beneficiary's Medicare number and bank information. Some versions threaten that if the cap is not activated by a deadline, the beneficiary loses it for the plan year.

The real rules

  • The $2,100 cap applies by law. No action is required to receive it. The plan tracks your running total and tells you when you have hit the cap.
  • Once you hit $2,100 in true out-of-pocket costs for the calendar year, you pay $0 for covered Part D drugs for the rest of the year.
  • If you want to spread the $2,100 across the year rather than pay it in a lump during a single costly month, you can voluntarily opt into the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan (M3P) — through your plan directly, never through a cold caller. M3P has no fee either.

Scam 3: The "Your Plan Is Changing — Act Now" Pressure Call

The oldest AEP scam uses urgency. The caller tells the beneficiary that their current plan is being discontinued, that benefits are ending, that premiums are jumping unless they switch within 24 hours. The caller then offers to "handle the transfer" if the beneficiary confirms their Medicare number, date of birth, and sometimes a bank account.

What's actually happening with 2027 plans

Every Medicare Advantage and Part D plan sends an Annual Notice of Change (ANOC) by September 30 that spells out exactly what is changing for 2027 — premiums, deductibles, formulary, copays, provider network. Changes do happen, sometimes big ones. But they are documented in writing, mailed to you, and effective January 1 — not activated by a phone call that pressures you to decide today.

The CMS rule that catches this scam every time

CMS prohibits agents from making superlative or urgency claims. "You must switch now or lose your benefits," "This is the best plan available," "If you don't act today you'll pay more" are all prohibited statements for licensed Medicare agents. Any caller using that language is either a scammer or a licensed agent in direct violation of CMS rules — either way, you hang up.

Scam 4: The "Free Equipment or Gift Card for Verifying Your Medicare Number" Offer

The caller offers a free back brace, free DNA testing kit, free diabetic supplies, free COVID test kit, or a $100 gift card — in exchange for "verifying your Medicare number so we can ship it." Sometimes the equipment is real and ships; the scam is that the sender then bills Medicare thousands of dollars for durable medical equipment the beneficiary did not need, often repeatedly over months. Sometimes the gift card never arrives and the Medicare number is used for identity theft.

What CMS actually allows

A licensed agent can offer a nominal promotional item worth up to $15 fair market value, cannot condition it on enrollment, and must offer it to anyone who attends an event regardless of whether they sign up. Anything above $15, anything conditioned on enrollment, anything paired with "give me your Medicare number first" is a direct violation.

Your Medicare number is a payment credential. Treat it like a credit card number. The only places that legitimately need it are: your providers at the time of service, your plan when you call its member services line, and Medicare itself when you call 1-800-MEDICARE. A random inbound caller does not qualify.

The CMS Marketing Rules Real Agents Follow in 2026

Licensed Medicare agents and third-party marketing organizations (TPMOs) operate under a tight CMS rulebook. Knowing the rules makes scam calls easier to spot because scammers break them openly.

Rule What a real agent must do What a scammer typically does
Contact permission No cold calls, texts, or door-knocks. Beneficiary must opt in first. Cold-calls out of the blue.
Marketing window Cannot market 2027 plans before October 1, 2026. Calls about "2027 benefits" in August and September.
TPMO disclaimer Reads a specific CMS disclaimer at the start of marketing calls. Opens with urgency or a false identity.
Scope of Appointment Obtains a signed SOA at least 48 hours before a sales appointment listing exact products. Tries to sell on the first call.
Gifts Up to $15 fair market value, not conditioned on enrollment. Offers gift cards, free equipment, or meals contingent on signup.
Superlative claims Cannot say "best plan," "only plan," "must act now." Opens with exactly those phrases.

CMS enforces average civil monetary penalties of $100,000+ per marketing violation; 100% of marketing materials must be filed with CMS.

The 60-Second Phone Checklist

When any call about Medicare, your plan, your prescriptions, or new benefits comes in, run this checklist. Each step takes under ten seconds.

  1. Did I initiate this call? If no, strong presumption of scam.
  2. Does the caller claim to be Medicare, Social Security, or your drug plan? If yes, hang up — those organizations do not call unsolicited.
  3. Is there urgency language? "Act now," "deadline," "losing benefits." Red flag.
  4. Are they asking for your Medicare number, SSN, date of birth, or bank information? Red flag unless you are the one who dialed them from a verified line.
  5. Are they offering something free (equipment, gift card, meal) in exchange for information? Red flag.
  6. Is a licensed agent reading the TPMO disclaimer? If they claim to be an agent and skip it, they are either a scammer or a licensed agent in violation. Hang up.
  7. Are they asking you to decide today? You never have to decide a plan change on a phone call. AEP runs until December 7. SEPs and other windows have their own rules.

If any one of those seven items trips, hang up without apology. Call 1-800-MEDICARE if you want to verify anything the caller claimed.

What to Do in the Next 24 Hours If You Already Shared Information

If a call feels wrong after it ends, assume it was and move fast. Response speed matters a lot more than certainty.

  1. Call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227). Report the call, ask about any recent activity on your Medicare number, and ask whether a new Medicare number can be issued if there is evidence of misuse. CMS can replace a compromised number.
  2. Call the Senior Medicare Patrol (SMP) in your state at 1-877-808-2468 or at smpresource.org. SMP volunteers are trained to walk beneficiaries through a scam response and to pass intelligence to law enforcement.
  3. File a complaint with the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov. If a bank account or payment was shared, this is also the step that triggers broader fraud alerts.
  4. Alert your bank or card issuer if any payment information was shared. Request new card numbers.
  5. Review your Medicare Summary Notice (Original Medicare) or your Part D Explanation of Benefits over the next 90 days for charges you don't recognize. Dispute each one with the biller first, then with Medicare.
  6. Place a fraud alert on your credit file at one of the three bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion). One call to any of the three triggers alerts at all three.

Doing AEP the Right Way

The point of knowing the scams is not to avoid shopping plans. AEP is a real window and the stakes are real — a wrong Part D plan can cost thousands in a year, a Medicare Advantage change can mean losing access to a specialist you need. The goal is to shop on your own terms, from inputs you control.

Where to actually compare 2027 plans

  • Medicare Plan Finder at medicare.gov/plan-compare. Enter your ZIP, your drug list, and your preferred pharmacy — the comparison runs on CMS's own data and shows every plan available to you.
  • Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) at shiphelp.org. SHIP counselors are free, trained, and prohibited from selling anything. They walk through plan options one-on-one.
  • A licensed Medicare agent you contacted first. Reach out through a known agency's website, confirm their NPN against the National Insurance Producer Registry (nipr.com), and only share information after you have verified them.

Decisions you can make before December 7

  • Stay in your current Medicare Advantage plan, or switch to a different MA plan.
  • Drop Medicare Advantage and go back to Original Medicare (and add a standalone Part D plan).
  • Keep Original Medicare and switch Part D plans.
  • Add or drop a Medigap policy (subject to underwriting outside of your initial enrollment or a guaranteed-issue window).

The Short Version

Medicare never calls you first. The $2,100 Part D cap is automatic — no activation, no fee. AI voice clones are real in 2026 and sound like family members and pharmacists; the only reliable verification is hanging up and calling a number you already trust. CMS rules bar unsolicited contact, urgency language, superlative claims, gifts over $15, and plan sales without a prior Scope of Appointment — so if any of those show up on a call, you are talking to a scammer or an agent breaking the rules, and either way the response is the same. Shop AEP through Medicare Plan Finder, your state SHIP counselor, or an agent you dialed first after verifying their license. If you think you shared anything with a scammer, call 1-800-MEDICARE, your state SMP, and the FTC within 24 hours and watch your Medicare notices for 90 days.
